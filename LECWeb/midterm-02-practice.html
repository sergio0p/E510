Abel and Bella

The setting for every problem

Two consumers, Abel and Bella, live for three periods. There is no uncertainty. Each consumer has an endowment and a logarithmic utility.

Basic Mathematica Commands

Unconstrained Optimization

Problem 2

Assume contingent markets and normalize the price of present consumption to $p_0=1$. A consumer has endowment $e=(e_0,e_1,e_2)=(\eZero,\eOne,\eTwo)$.

Question 1
Write the budget constraint that a consumer with this endowment faces.
Answer 1
The value of consumption equals the value of the endowment: $$c_0+p_1c_1+p_2c_2=e_0+p_1e_1+p_2e_2=\income.$$
Question 2
The discounted utility is $$\ln c_0+\delta\ln c_1+\delta^2\ln c_2.$$ Use the budget constraint to solve for $c_0$ in terms of $c_1$, $c_2$, $p_1$, and $p_2$, and write the discounted utility as a function of $c_1$, $c_2$, $p_1$, and $p_2$ only (that is, eliminate $c_0$).
Answer 2
Solving the budget constraint for $c_0$, $$c_0=\income-p_1c_1-p_2c_2,$$ so the discounted utility is $$U(c_1,c_2)=\ln\paren{\income-p_1c_1-p_2c_2}+\delta\ln c_1+\delta^2\ln c_2.$$
Question 3
Apply Recipe #0: what are the first-order conditions for $c_1$ and $c_2$?
Answer 3
The first-order conditions are $$\begin{aligned} \diff{U}{c_1}&=-\frac{p_1}{\income-p_1c_1-p_2c_2}+\frac{\delta}{c_1}=0,\\ \diff{U}{c_2}&=-\frac{p_2}{\income-p_1c_1-p_2c_2}+\frac{\delta^2}{c_2}=0. \end{aligned}$$
Question 4
Apply Recipe #0: what are the optimal choices of $c_1$ and $c_2$?
Answer 4
The common denominator is $c_0$, so the first-order conditions say $p_1c_1=\delta\,c_0$ and $p_2c_2=\delta^2c_0$. Substituting into the budget constraint, $(1+\delta+\delta^2)\,c_0=\income$, so $$c_1=\frac{\delta\,\paren{\income}}{(1+\delta+\delta^2)\,p_1},\qquad c_2=\frac{\delta^2\paren{\income}}{(1+\delta+\delta^2)\,p_2}.$$

Optimization with Equality Constraints

Problem 3

Same problem as in Problem 2, with endowment $e=(\eZero,\eOne,\eTwo)$. Apply Recipe #1.

Question 1
Write the corresponding Lagrangian.
Answer 1
$$\mathcal{L}=\ln c_0+\delta\ln c_1+\delta^2\ln c_2-\lambda\paren{c_0+p_1c_1+p_2c_2-\paren{\income}}.$$
Question 2
Obtain the first-order conditions for $c_0$, $c_1$, $c_2$, and $\lambda$.
Answer 2
The first-order conditions are $$\begin{aligned} \diff{\mathcal{L}}{c_0}&=\frac{1}{c_0}-\lambda=0,\\ \diff{\mathcal{L}}{c_1}&=\frac{\delta}{c_1}-\lambda p_1=0,\\ \diff{\mathcal{L}}{c_2}&=\frac{\delta^2}{c_2}-\lambda p_2=0,\\ \diff{\mathcal{L}}{\lambda}&=-\paren{c_0+p_1c_1+p_2c_2-\paren{\income}}=0. \end{aligned}$$
Question 3
If you substitute the values of $c_1$ and $c_2$ you obtained in Problem 2, what values do you obtain for $c_0$ and $\lambda$?
Answer 3
The first-order condition for $c_1$ gives $\lambda=\dfrac{\delta}{p_1c_1}$. Substituting $c_1=\dfrac{\delta\,\paren{\income}}{(1+\delta+\delta^2)\,p_1}$ and then using the first-order condition for $c_0$, $c_0=\dfrac{1}{\lambda}$: $$\lambda=\frac{1+\delta+\delta^2}{\income},\qquad c_0=\frac{\income}{1+\delta+\delta^2}.$$ The value of $c_2$ gives the same $\lambda$, and the budget constraint holds: $p_1c_1+p_2c_2=(\delta+\delta^2)\,c_0$, so $c_0+p_1c_1+p_2c_2=(1+\delta+\delta^2)\,c_0=\income$.

Optimization with Inequality Constraints

Problem 4

Same as Problem 3, with endowment $e=(\eZero,\eOne,\eTwo)$, but now we do not assume that the budget constraint holds with equality: $$c_0+p_1c_1+p_2c_2\le\income.$$

Question 1
If, in a utility maximization problem, the solution is such that the budget constraint is slack, $c_0+p_1c_1+p_2c_2\lt\income$, what can we say about $\lambda$?
  1. $\lambda=0$
  2. $\lambda\lt 0$
  3. $\lambda\gt 0$
Answer 1
(a) $\lambda=0$.
Question 2
Applying Recipe #2 and assuming $\lambda=0$, what are the corresponding first-order conditions?
Answer 2
Setting $\lambda=0$ in the Lagrangian leaves the discounted utility, and the variables are $c_0$, $c_1$, and $c_2$: $$\begin{aligned} \diff{\mathcal{L}}{c_0}&=\frac{1}{c_0}=0,\\ \diff{\mathcal{L}}{c_1}&=\frac{\delta}{c_1}=0,\\ \diff{\mathcal{L}}{c_2}&=\frac{\delta^2}{c_2}=0. \end{aligned}$$
Question 3
Applying Recipe #2, why will $\lambda=0$ never correspond to a solution of this problem? Please use the first-order conditions in your argument.
Answer 3
For every $c_0\gt 0$, $\frac{1}{c_0}\gt 0$, so the first condition never holds (and neither do the other two, since $\frac{\delta}{c_1}\gt 0$ and $\frac{\delta^2}{c_2}\gt 0$). The case $\lambda=0$ has no solution, so by Recipe #2 we must consider $\lambda\gt 0$: the budget constraint binds. The marginal utility of consumption is always positive, so the consumer never leaves income unspent.

The Value Function and the Envelope Trick

Problem 5

Question 1
What is the value function of the consumer's maximization problem with the same endowment as before, $e=(\eZero,\eOne,\eTwo)$? Notice that it should be a function of $p_1$ and $p_2$.
Answer 1
Substitute the optimal choices from Problems 2 and 3 into the discounted utility: $$V(p_1,p_2)=\ln\frac{\income}{1+\delta+\delta^2}+\delta\ln\frac{\delta\,\paren{\income}}{(1+\delta+\delta^2)\,p_1}+\delta^2\ln\frac{\delta^2\paren{\income}}{(1+\delta+\delta^2)\,p_2}.$$ Collecting the logarithms of income, $$V(p_1,p_2)=(1+\delta+\delta^2)\ln\frac{\income}{1+\delta+\delta^2}+(\delta+2\delta^2)\ln\delta-\delta\ln p_1-\delta^2\ln p_2.$$
Question 2
Using the Lagrangian you wrote in Problem 3, what is $\diff{\mathcal{L}(c_0,c_1,c_2,\lambda,p_1,p_2)}{p_1}$?
Answer 2
The price $p_1$ enters the Lagrangian twice: in the cost of $c_1$ and in the value of the endowment $e_1=\eOne$. So $$\diff{\mathcal{L}}{p_1}=-\lambda\paren{c_1-\eOne}=\lambda\paren{\eOne-c_1}.$$
Question 3
The expression $e_1-c_1$ is the individual net supply of good $c_1$, while $c_1-e_1$ is the net demand. Using the values of $\lambda$ and $c_1$ that we obtained in the previous questions and the given value of $e_1$, what is $\lambda\paren{e_1-c_1}$? Now compare this value with $\diff{V}{p_1}$, where $V$ is the value function you obtained in question 1.
Answer 3
With $\lambda=\frac{1+\delta+\delta^2}{\income}$ and $c_1=\frac{\delta\,\paren{\income}}{(1+\delta+\delta^2)\,p_1}$, $$\lambda\paren{e_1-c_1}=\frac{1+\delta+\delta^2}{\income}\paren{\eOne-\frac{\delta\,\paren{\income}}{(1+\delta+\delta^2)\,p_1}}=\frac{(1+\delta+\delta^2)\,\eOne}{\income}-\frac{\delta}{p_1}.$$ Differentiating the value function of question 1, $$\diff{V}{p_1}=(1+\delta+\delta^2)\,\frac{\eOne}{\income}-\frac{\delta}{p_1}.$$ The two are equal. This is the envelope trick: the derivative of the value function with respect to $p_1$ equals the partial derivative of the Lagrangian, evaluated at the solution.
Question 4
Remember that $\lambda$ is the marginal value of income and $\diff{V}{p_1}$ is the marginal effect on utility of an increase in the price $p_1$ of good $c_1$. Relate this to whether the individual is a net supplier of good $c_1$, $e_1-c_1\gt 0$, or a net demander of good $c_1$, $c_1-e_1\gt 0$.
Answer 4
By the envelope trick, $\diff{V}{p_1}=\lambda\paren{e_1-c_1}$, and $\lambda\gt 0$ (Problem 4). A rise $dp_1$ in the price changes the value of what the individual sells or buys of good $c_1$ by $\paren{e_1-c_1}dp_1$, and $\lambda$ converts that change in income into utility. So:
  • A net supplier ($e_1-c_1\gt 0$) gains from a higher $p_1$: $\diff{V}{p_1}\gt 0$. The units it sells are worth more.
  • A net demander ($c_1-e_1\gt 0$) loses from a higher $p_1$: $\diff{V}{p_1}\lt 0$. The units it buys cost more.
  • If $c_1=e_1$, a small change in $p_1$ has no first-order effect on utility.

Contingent Markets

Problem 6

Abel has the same endowment as before, $e^A=(\eZero,\eOne,\eTwo)$, and Bella has endowment $e^B=(\bZero,\bOne,\bTwo)$. Both have discounted utility $\ln c_0+\delta\ln c_1+\delta^2\ln c_2$. With prices $(1,p_1,p_2)$, their demands are $$\begin{aligned} c_0^A&=\frac{\income}{1+\delta+\delta^2}, & c_1^A&=\frac{\delta\paren{\income}}{(1+\delta+\delta^2)\,p_1}, & c_2^A&=\frac{\delta^2\paren{\income}}{(1+\delta+\delta^2)\,p_2},\\ c_0^B&=\frac{\incomeB}{1+\delta+\delta^2}, & c_1^B&=\frac{\delta\paren{\incomeB}}{(1+\delta+\delta^2)\,p_1}, & c_2^B&=\frac{\delta^2\paren{\incomeB}}{(1+\delta+\delta^2)\,p_2}. \end{aligned}$$

Question 1
Write the market clearing conditions for the markets for $c_0$ and for $c_1$.
Answer 1
Market for $c_0$, $c_0^A+c_0^B=e_0^A+e_0^B$: $$\frac{\income}{1+\delta+\delta^2}+\frac{\incomeB}{1+\delta+\delta^2}=\eZero+\bZero\iff\frac{\incomeTot}{1+\delta+\delta^2}=\totZero.$$ Market for $c_1$, $c_1^A+c_1^B=e_1^A+e_1^B$: $$\frac{\delta\paren{\incomeTot}}{(1+\delta+\delta^2)\,p_1}=\totOne.$$
Question 2
What are the equilibrium values of $p_1$ and $p_2$?
Answer 2
The market for $c_0$ gives $\incomeTot=\totZero\,(1+\delta+\delta^2)$. Substituting into the market for $c_1$, $$\frac{\totZero\,\delta}{p_1}=\totOne\iff p_1=\frac{\totZero\,\delta}{\totOne}=\pOneEq.$$ Back in the market for $c_0$, $\totTwo\,p_2=\totZero\,(\delta+\delta^2)-\totOne\,p_1=\totZero\,\delta^2$, so $$p_2=\frac{\totZero\,\delta^2}{\totTwo}=\pTwoEq.$$ The market for $c_2$ then clears by Walras' law.

Efficiency

Problem 7

Now there are only two periods, $t=0,1$. Abel's endowment is $e^A=(\effX,\effY)$ and Bella's is $e^B=(\effY,\effX)$: the endowments are symmetric, and the total endowment is $\effT$ in both periods. Both have discounted utility $\ln c_0+\delta\ln c_1$.

To simplify things, we write Bella's consumption as the residual of the total endowment minus Abel's consumption: $$c_0^B=\effT-c_0^A,\qquad c_1^B=\effT-c_1^A.$$

Question 1
Let $u^A=\ln c_0^A+\delta\ln c_1^A$ and $u^B=\ln c_0^B+\delta\ln c_1^B$ be Abel's and Bella's discounted utilities. Which of the following can be considered welfare functions? (Mark all that apply.)
  1. $W(u^A,u^B)=u^A-u^B$
  2. $W(u^A,u^B)=u^A+u^B$
  3. $W(u^A,u^B)=\min\set{u^A,u^B}$
  4. $W(u^A,u^B)=u^A\cdot u^B$
  5. $W(u^A,u^B)=\tfrac13\,u^A+\tfrac23\,u^B$
  6. $W(u^A,u^B)=u^A$
Answer 1
Correct: (b) and (e). A welfare function must be strictly increasing in every player's utility, $\diff{W}{u^A}\gt 0$ and $\diff{W}{u^B}\gt 0$, and both sums are.
  • (a) decreases with Bella's utility: $\diff{W}{u^B}=-1$.
  • (c) does not change with the larger of the two utilities.
  • (d) has $\diff{W}{u^A}=u^B$, which is negative whenever Bella's utility is negative, as it is when her consumption is below $1$ in both periods.
  • (f) ignores Bella: $\diff{W}{u^B}=0$.
Question 2
Take the welfare function $W=\tfrac12\,u^A+\tfrac12\,u^B$. Since Bella's consumption is the residual, everything is a function of Abel's consumption $\paren{c_0^A,c_1^A}$. Use Recipe #0 to maximize $W$ by choosing Abel's consumption.
Answer 2
Substituting $c_t^B=\effT-c_t^A$, $$W\paren{c_0^A,c_1^A}=\tfrac12\paren{\ln c_0^A+\delta\ln c_1^A}+\tfrac12\paren{\ln\paren{\effT-c_0^A}+\delta\ln\paren{\effT-c_1^A}}.$$ The first-order conditions are $$\begin{aligned} \diff{W}{c_0^A}&=\tfrac12\paren{\frac{1}{c_0^A}-\frac{1}{\effT-c_0^A}}=0,\\ \diff{W}{c_1^A}&=\tfrac12\,\delta\paren{\frac{1}{c_1^A}-\frac{1}{\effT-c_1^A}}=0. \end{aligned}$$ Each condition says $c_t^A=\effT-c_t^A$, and $\delta$ cancels from the second. So $$c_0^A=c_1^A=\effHalf,\qquad c_0^B=c_1^B=\effHalf:$$ Abel and Bella consume the same amount, half of the total endowment, in each period.
Question 3
What is the value of the players' utility at this symmetric efficient point?
Answer 3
Both consume $\effHalf$ in each period, so $$u^A=u^B=\ln\paren{\effHalf}+\delta\ln\paren{\effHalf}=(1+\delta)\ln\paren{\effHalf}.$$
Question 4
Maximize Abel's utility subject to Bella's utility being greater than or equal to its level at the symmetric efficient point: $$\max_{c_0^A,c_1^A}\;\ln c_0^A+\delta\ln c_1^A\quad\st\quad\ln\paren{\effT-c_0^A}+\delta\ln\paren{\effT-c_1^A}\ge(1+\delta)\ln\paren{\effHalf}.$$ Before you write the Lagrangian, write the constraint in the form that Recipe #2 requires: a function less than or equal to a constant. Then write the Lagrangian and the first-order conditions.
Answer 4
Multiplying the constraint by $-1$ turns it into a function less than or equal to a constant: $$-\ln\paren{\effT-c_0^A}-\delta\ln\paren{\effT-c_1^A}\le-(1+\delta)\ln\paren{\effHalf}.$$ The Lagrangian is $$\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}&=\ln c_0^A+\delta\ln c_1^A-\lambda\paren{-\ln\paren{\effT-c_0^A}-\delta\ln\paren{\effT-c_1^A}+(1+\delta)\ln\paren{\effHalf}}\\ &=\ln c_0^A+\delta\ln c_1^A+\lambda\paren{\ln\paren{\effT-c_0^A}+\delta\ln\paren{\effT-c_1^A}-(1+\delta)\ln\paren{\effHalf}}. \end{aligned}$$ The first-order conditions are $$\begin{aligned} \diff{\mathcal{L}}{c_0^A}&=\frac{1}{c_0^A}-\frac{\lambda}{\effT-c_0^A}=0,\\ \diff{\mathcal{L}}{c_1^A}&=\frac{\delta}{c_1^A}-\frac{\lambda\,\delta}{\effT-c_1^A}=0, \end{aligned}$$ and, in the case $\lambda\gt 0$, $$\diff{\mathcal{L}}{\lambda}=\ln\paren{\effT-c_0^A}+\delta\ln\paren{\effT-c_1^A}-(1+\delta)\ln\paren{\effHalf}=0.$$
Question 5
Argue verbally that the solution of the maximization problem in question 4 has to be an efficient allocation. Use the maximization problem, but also the fact that the utility functions have positive marginal utilities, so more is always better.
Answer 5
Suppose the solution were not efficient. Then some other feasible allocation makes one consumer better off without making the other worse off. There are two cases.
  • Abel is better off and Bella is not worse off. Bella's utility is still at least $(1+\delta)\ln\paren{\effHalf}$, so this allocation satisfies the constraint and gives Abel more utility. That contradicts the allocation being the solution of the maximization problem.
  • Bella is better off and Abel is not worse off. Bella's utility is then strictly above the constraint level, so we can take a little consumption from Bella and give it to Abel while keeping her above that level. Since marginal utilities are positive, more is better and Abel's utility rises. Again Abel gets more utility within the constraint, a contradiction.
Either way we contradict the maximization, so the solution is efficient.
Question 6
Compare the first-order conditions of the two problems: the maximization of the welfare function $W=\tfrac12\,u^A+\tfrac12\,u^B$ with Recipe #0 (question 2), and the constrained maximization of Abel's utility (question 4).
Answer 6
Multiplied by $2$, the first-order conditions of question 2 are $$\frac{1}{c_0^A}-\frac{1}{\effT-c_0^A}=0,\qquad \frac{\delta}{c_1^A}-\frac{\delta}{\effT-c_1^A}=0.$$ Those of question 4 are the same, except that $\lambda$ multiplies Bella's marginal utilities: $$\frac{1}{c_0^A}-\frac{\lambda}{\effT-c_0^A}=0,\qquad \frac{\delta}{c_1^A}-\frac{\lambda\,\delta}{\effT-c_1^A}=0.$$ So the first-order conditions of question 4 are those of maximizing the welfare function $u^A+\lambda\,u^B$: the multiplier plays the role of Bella's welfare weight relative to Abel's. In both problems, the ratio of Abel's to Bella's marginal utility is the same in both periods. With $\lambda=1$ the two systems coincide. The symmetric point $c_0^A=c_1^A=\effHalf$ solves them and satisfies Bella's constraint with equality, so it is also the solution of question 4, with $\lambda=1$.

Financial Markets

Problem 8

Back to the three-period story, $t=0,1,2$, with Abel's endowment $e=(\eZero,\eOne,\eTwo)$. There are two financial assets:

  • Short-term bond $X$: pays $r_X$ per dollar invested from $t=0$ to $t=1$.
  • Long-term (zero-coupon) bond $Y$: pays $r_Y$ per dollar invested from $t=0$ to $t=2$.

Here $r_X$ and $r_Y$ are the gross rates of return of the two bonds. Let $z_X$ and $z_Y$ be the dollar amounts that Abel invests in each bond: $z\gt 0$ means Abel buys the bond, and $z\lt 0$ means Abel sells it.

Question 1
Write the three spot budget constraints, using $1$ as the price of consumption and $r_X$ and $r_Y$ as the returns of the bonds.
Answer 1
$$\begin{aligned} t=0:&\quad c_0+z_X+z_Y=\eZero,\\ t=1:&\quad c_1=\eOne+r_X\,z_X,\\ t=2:&\quad c_2=\eTwo+r_Y\,z_Y. \end{aligned}$$
Question 2
Use the spot budget constraints to write the discounted utility as a function of $z_X$ and $z_Y$ (eliminate $c_0$, $c_1$, and $c_2$).
Answer 2
$$U(z_X,z_Y)=\ln\paren{\eZero-z_X-z_Y}+\delta\ln\paren{\eOne+r_X\,z_X}+\delta^2\ln\paren{\eTwo+r_Y\,z_Y}.$$
Question 3
Use Recipe #0 and obtain the first-order conditions with respect to $z_X$ and $z_Y$.
Answer 3
$$\begin{aligned} \diff{U}{z_X}&=-\frac{1}{\eZero-z_X-z_Y}+\frac{\delta\,r_X}{\eOne+r_X\,z_X}=0,\\ \diff{U}{z_Y}&=-\frac{1}{\eZero-z_X-z_Y}+\frac{\delta^2\,r_Y}{\eTwo+r_Y\,z_Y}=0. \end{aligned}$$
Question 4
Solve the first-order conditions and obtain the asset demands $z_X$ and $z_Y$.
Answer 4
Write $c_0=\eZero-z_X-z_Y$, $c_1=\eOne+r_X\,z_X$, and $c_2=\eTwo+r_Y\,z_Y$. The first-order conditions say $c_1=\delta\,r_X\,c_0$ and $c_2=\delta^2\,r_Y\,c_0$, so $$z_X=\delta\,c_0-\frac{\eOne}{r_X},\qquad z_Y=\delta^2\,c_0-\frac{\eTwo}{r_Y}.$$ Substituting into $c_0=\eZero-z_X-z_Y$ gives $(1+\delta+\delta^2)\,c_0=\incomeFin$, so the asset demands are $$z_X=\frac{\delta\paren{\incomeFin}}{1+\delta+\delta^2}-\frac{\eOne}{r_X},\qquad z_Y=\frac{\delta^2\paren{\incomeFin}}{1+\delta+\delta^2}-\frac{\eTwo}{r_Y}.$$
Question 5
Now Abel and Bella trade the two bonds, with their endowments from Problem 6, $e^A=(\eZero,\eOne,\eTwo)$ and $e^B=(\bZero,\bOne,\bTwo)$. If the equilibrium return rate for asset $X$ is $r_X=\rXEq$, what must the equilibrium return rate for asset $Y$ be?
Answer 5
The two bonds replicate the contingent markets of Problem 6: a dollar invested in bond $X$ at $t=0$ buys $r_X$ units of $c_1$, so the price of $c_1$ in units of $c_0$ is $p_1=\frac{1}{r_X}$, and likewise $p_2=\frac{1}{r_Y}$. The equilibrium prices of Problem 6, $p_1=\pOneEq$ and $p_2=\pTwoEq$, give $r_X=\frac{1}{p_1}=\rXEq$, as stated, and $$r_Y=\frac{1}{p_2}=\rYEq.$$

The Financial Structure

Problem 9

Same economy as in Problem 8, with a third financial asset, bond $H$, traded at $t=1$:

  • Short-term bond $X$: pays $r_X$ per dollar invested from $t=0$ to $t=1$.
  • Short-term bond $H$: pays $r_H$ per dollar invested from $t=1$ to $t=2$.
  • Long-term (zero-coupon) bond $Y$: pays $r_Y$ per dollar invested from $t=0$ to $t=2$.

The returns are $r_X=\retX$, $r_Y=\retY$, and $r_H=\retH$.

Question 1
Let $w=(w_X,w_Y,w_H)$ be a portfolio. What is the payoff of the portfolio at $t=0$? At $t=1$? At $t=2$?
Answer 1
The returns of the assets are $\mathbf r_X=(-1,\retX,0)$, $\mathbf r_Y=(-1,0,\retY)$, and $\mathbf r_H=(0,-1,\retH)$, so the payoffs are $$\begin{aligned} t=0:&\quad -w_X-w_Y,\\ t=1:&\quad \retX\,w_X-w_H,\\ t=2:&\quad \retY\,w_Y+\retH\,w_H. \end{aligned}$$
Question 2
Can you find values of $w_X$, $w_Y$, and $w_H$ such that the payoff at $t=0$ is zero but the payoffs at $t=1$ and $t=2$ are positive?
Answer 2
Yes, because $r_X\,r_H=\prodXH\cmpXH\retY=r_Y$. For example, take $w=(w_X,w_Y,w_H)=(\wX,\wY,\wH)$. Its payoffs are $$\begin{aligned} t=0:&\quad \payZeroExpr,\\ t=1:&\quad \payOneExpr,\\ t=2:&\quad \payTwoExpr. \end{aligned}$$ Buy bond $X$ and sell bond $Y$ in equal dollar amounts, so nothing is paid at $t=0$; at $t=1$, reinvest most of bond $X$'s payoff in bond $H$. Since $r_X\,r_H\gt r_Y$, rolling over the two short-term bonds pays more than the long-term bond.Sell bond $X$ and buy bond $Y$ in equal dollar amounts, so nothing is paid at $t=0$; at $t=1$, sell bond $H$ to cover more than what bond $X$ costs. Since $r_X\,r_H\lt r_Y$, the long-term bond pays more than rolling over the two short-term bonds. Such a portfolio is an arbitrage opportunity.
