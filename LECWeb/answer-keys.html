Midterm 1 Answer Key

Question 1

The next several questions use the following public good setting.

There are two individuals, $i=1,2$. Individual $i$ has income $w_i$, contributes $g_i$ to a public good, and consumes the rest privately:

$$\begin{align*}x_i=w_i-g_i,\qquad G=g_1+g_2.\end{align*}$$

Individual $i$’s preferences are given by

$$\begin{align*}u_i(x_i,G)=x_i+G\left(4-\frac{G}{2}\right).\end{align*}$$

Substituting $x_i$ and $G$, we can write each individual’s utility as a function of the two contributions:

$$\begin{align*}u_i(g_1,g_2)=w_i-g_i+(g_1+g_2)\left(4-\frac{g_1+g_2}{2}\right).\end{align*}$$

Expanded:

$$\begin{align*} u_1(g_1,g_2)&=w_1+3g_1+4g_2-\frac{g_1^2}{2}-g_1g_2-\frac{g_2^2}{2}\\ u_2(g_1,g_2)&=w_2+4g_1+3g_2-\frac{g_1^2}{2}-g_1g_2-\frac{g_2^2}{2} \end{align*}$$

Note: a superscript is a power exponent and a subscript identifies the individual. In $g_1^2$, the subscript 1 means individual 1 and the superscript 2 means squared.

Midterm 1 Answer Key

Question 2

Using Mathematica syntax, which of the following correctly defines the payoff function $u_1(x_1,G)=x_1+G\cdot\left(4-\frac{G}{2}\right)$?

Answer. u1[x1_, G_] := x1 + G*(4 - G/2)

The arguments of a Mathematica function go in square brackets, and on the left-hand side each argument carries an underscore (x1_, G_) so that it stands for any value. The right-hand side uses the plain names, without underscores. Terms are grouped with parentheses, never square brackets, and G*(4 - G/2) keeps the division by 2 inside the parentheses, as in $G\left(4-\frac{G}{2}\right)$. Each of the other options breaks at least one of these rules.

Question 3

Ignoring the constraints $0\le g_1\le w_1$, use Recipe #0 and write down player 1’s best response to $g_2$. Please write only player 1’s best response; do not include your reasoning or work.

Answer. $BR_1(g_2)=3-g_2$.

Recipe #0 sets the derivative of $u_1$ with respect to player 1’s choice equal to zero:

$$\begin{align*} \frac{\partial u_1}{\partial g_1}&=3-g_1-g_2=0,\\ g_1&=3-g_2. \end{align*}$$

Midterm 1 Answer Key

Question 4

Assume wealth levels are $w_1=5$ and $w_2=7$. In any Nash equilibrium, what is the total level of public good?

Hint: unlike the classroom examples, we may have many Nash equilibria here.

Answer. $G=3$.

Player 2’s best response comes from the same recipe:

$$\begin{align*} \frac{\partial u_2}{\partial g_2}&=3-g_1-g_2=0,\\ BR_2(g_1)&=3-g_1. \end{align*}$$

Both best responses describe the same line,

$$\begin{align*}g_1+g_2=3,\end{align*}$$

so every pair $(g_1,g_2)$ on this line with $0\le g_1\le 3$ is a Nash equilibrium, and in each of them $G=g_1+g_2=3$. The wealth bounds never bind, because no contribution exceeds $3$, which is less than both $w_1=5$ and $w_2=7$.

Question 5

Now assume there is only one individual, so $g_2=0$, but there are two periods, $t=0$ and $t=1$. Let $(g_0,g_1)$ be the individual’s contributions in periods 0 and 1.

The public good is a flow: it does not carry over to the next period, so $G_t=g_t$. In each period $t$, the individual consumes $x_t=w_t-g_t$ privately and has utility $x_t+G_t\left(4-\frac{G_t}{2}\right)$.

Wealth can be transferred costlessly over time, and total wealth is 9:

$$\begin{align*}w_0+w_1=9.\end{align*}$$

The individual chooses $(g_0,g_1,w_0,w_1)$ to maximize discounted utility subject to the total wealth constraint.

Question 6

Write explicitly the agent’s discounted utility for this problem as a function of the choice variables and the discount factor. Use $d$ to denote the discount factor.

Notice: “explicitly” means the expression may contain only $g_0$, $g_1$, $w_0$, $w_1$, $d$, and numbers. Do not leave any function or shorthand symbols such as $u(\cdot)$, $x_t$, or $G_t$ in your answer.

Answer.

$$\begin{align*} U&=w_0-g_0+g_0\left(4-\frac{g_0}{2}\right)+d\left(w_1-g_1+g_1\left(4-\frac{g_1}{2}\right)\right). \end{align*}$$

Midterm 1 Answer Key

Question 7

Consider the Lagrangian for the above problem. Obtain the first-order condition of the Lagrangian with respect to $\lambda$.

Hint: remember that a first-order condition entails a bit more than just computing a derivative.

Answer. With $U$ the discounted utility of Question 6 and the constraint $w_0+w_1=9$, the Lagrangian is

$$\begin{align*}\mathcal{L}=U(g_0,g_1,w_0,w_1)-\lambda\,(w_0+w_1-9),\end{align*}$$

and its first-order condition with respect to $\lambda$ sets the derivative equal to zero:

$$\begin{align*} \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\lambda}&=-(w_0+w_1-9)=0,\\ \text{that is,}\quad w_0+w_1&=9. \end{align*}$$

The first-order condition with respect to the multiplier recovers the constraint.

Question 8

We are back to the static setting of Question 1, with two players. Player 1 chooses $g_1$ to maximize $u_1(g_1,g_2)$ subject to two constraints:

$$\begin{align*} \max_{g_1}\;&w_1+3g_1+4g_2-\frac{g_1^2}{2}-g_1g_2-\frac{g_2^2}{2}\\ \text{subject to}\;&0\le g_1\quad\text{(first constraint)}\\ &g_1\le w_1\quad\text{(second constraint)} \end{align*}$$

Midterm 1 Answer Key

Question 9

Which constraint should we rewrite in order to apply Recipe #2?

Answer. The first constraint.

Recipe #2 needs every constraint written as a function of the choice variables less than or equal to a constant. The second constraint, $g_1\le w_1$, already has that form. The first, $0\le g_1$, does not, and is rewritten as

$$\begin{align*}-g_1\le 0.\end{align*}$$

Midterm 1 Answer Key

Question 10

In the case $\lambda_1=0$ and $\lambda_2\gt 0$, write down the corresponding first-order conditions that we must consider.

Answer. With the constraints written as $-g_1\le 0$ and $g_1\le w_1$, the Lagrangian is

$$\begin{align*}\mathcal{L}=u_1(g_1,g_2)-\lambda_1\,(-g_1)-\lambda_2\,(g_1-w_1).\end{align*}$$

In this case $\lambda_1=0$ drops out, and Recipe #1 is applied with the variables $g_1$ and $\lambda_2$:

$$\begin{align*} \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial g_1}&=3-g_1-g_2-\lambda_2=0,\\ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\lambda_2}&=-(g_1-w_1)=0,\quad\text{that is,}\quad g_1=w_1. \end{align*}$$

A solution in this case must also satisfy $\lambda_2\gt 0$ and the first constraint, $-g_1\le 0$.

Midterm 1 Answer Key

Question 11

If $g_2=2$ and $w_1=2$, does the case $\lambda_1=0$ and $\lambda_2\gt 0$ correspond to a solution? Why or why not?

Answer. No. The first-order conditions of Question 10 give

$$\begin{align*} g_1&=w_1=2,\\ \lambda_2&=3-g_1-g_2=3-2-2=-1\lt 0, \end{align*}$$

which contradicts $\lambda_2\gt 0$. The solution is interior instead: $g_1=3-g_2=1$, which lies between $0$ and $w_1=2$.

Question 1

The residents of a small oil town depend on a well that a company is drilling nearby. There are three states of the world: the well is empty ($e$), the oil field is modest ($m$), or the oil field is large ($l$). The three states are equally likely: $\Pr(e)=\Pr(m)=\Pr(l)=\frac13$. In period zero, drilling has just started, and the residents have no information about the state. In period one, the drill reaches the reservoir, and the residents learn whether the well is empty or not. In period two, no further news arrives: whether the field is modest or large will be known only once production starts, after period two. The residents discount the future with discount factor $\delta=0.9$.

Notation: $c_{ts}$ is consumption in period $t$ when the information set $s$ has been reached.

Which of the following is the residents’ discounted expected utility?

  • $u(c_0)+0.9\,u(c_1)+0.81\,u(c_2)$
  • $u(c_0)+0.9\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{1e})+\tfrac23u(c_{1ml})}+0.81\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{2e})+\tfrac13u(c_{2m})+\tfrac13u(c_{2l})}$
  • $u(c_0)+0.9\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{1e})+\tfrac23u(c_{1ml})}+0.9\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{2e})+\tfrac23u(c_{2ml})}$
  • $u(c_0)+0.9\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{1e})+\tfrac23u(c_{1ml})}+0.81\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{2e})+\tfrac23u(c_{2ml})}$
  • $u(c_0)+0.9\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{1l})+\tfrac23u(c_{1em})}+0.81\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{2l})+\tfrac23u(c_{2em})}$
  • $u(c_0)+0.9\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{1e})+\tfrac23u(c_{1ml})}$
  • $u(c_0)+0.9\paren{u(c_{1e})+u(c_{1ml})}+0.81\paren{u(c_{2e})+u(c_{2ml})}$
  • $u(c_0)+0.9\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{1e})+\tfrac23u(c_{1ml})}+0.81\paren{\tfrac23u(c_{2em})+\tfrac13u(c_{2l})}$
  • $u(c_0)+0.9\paren{\tfrac23u(c_{1e})+\tfrac13u(c_{1ml})}+0.81\paren{\tfrac23u(c_{2e})+\tfrac13u(c_{2ml})}$
  • $u(c_0)+0.9\,u(c_1)+0.81\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{2e})+\tfrac23u(c_{2ml})}$
  • $u(c_0)+0.9\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{1e})+\tfrac23u(c_{1ml})}+0.81\,u(c_2)$
  • $0.9\,u(c_0)+0.81\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{1e})+\tfrac23u(c_{1ml})}+0.729\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{2e})+\tfrac23u(c_{2ml})}$
  • $u(c_0)+0.9\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{1e})+\tfrac23u(c_{1ml})}+0.81\paren{\tfrac23u(c_{2el})+\tfrac13u(c_{2m})}$
  • $u(c_0)+0.9\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{1e})+\tfrac13u(c_{1m})+\tfrac13u(c_{1l})}+0.81\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{2e})+\tfrac13u(c_{2m})+\tfrac13u(c_{2l})}$
Answer 1

Answer. $u(c_0)+0.9\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{1e})+\tfrac23u(c_{1ml})}+0.81\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{2e})+\tfrac23u(c_{2ml})}$.

The information partitions are $\mathcal P_0=\set{\set{e,m,l}}$, $\mathcal P_1=\set{\set{e},\set{m,l}}$, and $\mathcal P_2=\set{\set{e},\set{m,l}}$: in period one the residents learn whether the well is empty, and in period two they learn nothing new. Each information set gets its probability, $\Pr(\set{e})=\frac13$ and $\Pr(\set{m,l})=\frac23$, and period $t$ is discounted by $\delta^t$: $1$, $0.9$, and $0.81$. The Information Partitions app builds this sum step by step.

  • $u(c_0)+0.9\,u(c_1)+0.81\,u(c_2)$. Incorrect. Consumption never depends on the news: it ignores that the residents learn in period one whether the well is empty.
  • $\ldots+0.81\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{2e})+\tfrac13u(c_{2m})+\tfrac13u(c_{2l})}$. Incorrect. It has the residents tell $m$ from $l$ in period two, but no news arrives in period two.
  • $\ldots+0.9\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{2e})+\tfrac23u(c_{2ml})}$. Incorrect. Period two is discounted by $\delta^2=0.81$, not by $\delta=0.9$.
  • $u(c_0)+0.9\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{1e})+\tfrac23u(c_{1ml})}+0.81\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{2e})+\tfrac23u(c_{2ml})}$. Correct. The right information sets in each period, weighted by their probabilities and discounted by $1$, $\delta$, and $\delta^2$.
  • $u(c_0)+0.9\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{1l})+\tfrac23u(c_{1em})}+\ldots$ Incorrect. The residents learn whether the well is empty, not whether the field is large: the information sets are $\set{e}$ and $\set{m,l}$, not $\set{l}$ and $\set{e,m}$.
  • $u(c_0)+0.9\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{1e})+\tfrac23u(c_{1ml})}$. Incorrect. Period two is missing.
  • $u(c_0)+0.9\paren{u(c_{1e})+u(c_{1ml})}+\ldots$ Incorrect. The probabilities are missing: in each period the weights add up to $2$ instead of $1$.
  • $\ldots+0.81\paren{\tfrac23u(c_{2em})+\tfrac13u(c_{2l})}$. Incorrect. In period two the residents still know whether the well is empty, so $e$ cannot share an information set with $m$.
  • $u(c_0)+0.9\paren{\tfrac23u(c_{1e})+\tfrac13u(c_{1ml})}+\ldots$ Incorrect. The probabilities are swapped: $\Pr(\set{e})=\frac13$ and $\Pr(\set{m,l})=\frac23$.
  • $u(c_0)+0.9\,u(c_1)+0.81\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{2e})+\tfrac23u(c_{2ml})}$. Incorrect. The news arrives in period one, so period-one consumption can already depend on it.
  • $\ldots+0.81\,u(c_2)$. Incorrect. Information is never forgotten: in period two the residents still know whether the well is empty.
  • $0.9\,u(c_0)+0.81\paren{\ldots}+0.729\paren{\ldots}$. Incorrect. Period zero is today and is not discounted: the discount factors are $1$, $\delta$, and $\delta^2$.
  • $\ldots+0.81\paren{\tfrac23u(c_{2el})+\tfrac13u(c_{2m})}$. Incorrect. In period two the residents still know whether the well is empty, so $e$ cannot share an information set with $l$.
  • $u(c_0)+0.9\paren{\tfrac13u(c_{1e})+\tfrac13u(c_{1m})+\tfrac13u(c_{1l})}+\ldots$ Incorrect. In period one the residents learn only whether the well is empty; they cannot tell $m$ from $l$ in either period.

Question 3

The next questions use the following setting.

Consider an economy with one period and two states of the world, $s_1$ and $s_2$, with probabilities $\Pr(s_1)=\frac13$ and $\Pr(s_2)=\frac23$. There is one consumption good. Contingent goods are traded in period zero, before the agents learn which state the economy is in; consumption takes place after they learn the state. Alice’s endowment is $e^A=(2,6)$ and Ben’s endowment is $e^B=(8,2)$, where the first entry is the endowment in state $s_1$ and the second is the endowment in state $s_2$. Both consumers have utility $u(c)=\ln c$, so a consumer’s expected utility is

$$\frac13\ln c_1+\frac23\ln c_2,$$

where $c_1$ and $c_2$ are consumption in states $s_1$ and $s_2$.

Question 4

Question 4

Assume there are contingent markets, and let $p_k$ be the price of a contract that delivers one unit of consumption in state $s_k$. We normalize prices so that $p_1=1$. Write down the budget constraint equation for Alice. Feel free to add any reasoning or work.

Answer 4

Answer.

$$c_1+p_2\,c_2=2+6p_2.$$

With contingent markets, Alice has a single budget constraint, written in period zero, before the state is known: the value of her consumption basket at prices $(p_1,p_2)=(1,p_2)$ equals the value of her endowment $e^A=(2,6)$. Writing it with $\le$ is also correct.

Common mistakes:

  • Probabilities do not enter the budget constraint, and neither does the utility function.
  • It must be Alice’s budget, with her endowment $(2,6)$: neither the generic $e_1+p_2e_2$ nor the total endowment $10+8p_2$.
  • $2+6p_2$ alone is Alice’s income, not her budget constraint: a budget constraint needs an equality or an inequality, cost of the basket $=$ income or cost of the basket $\le$ income.
  • Market-clearing conditions (supply $=$ demand) are not budget constraints (value of the basket $=$ value of the endowment for one consumer).
  • There is no $c_0$: consumption takes place only after the state is learned.

Question 5

Question 5

Use Recipe #1 and obtain Alice’s first-order conditions with respect to $c_1$ and $c_2$.

Calculus reminder: the derivative of $\ln x$ is $\frac1x$.

Answer 5

Answer. Recipe #1 maximizes the Lagrangian, with the budget constraint of Question 4:

$$\mathcal{L}=\tfrac13\ln c_1+\tfrac23\ln c_2-\lambda\paren{c_1+p_2c_2-2-6p_2}.$$

The first-order conditions are

$$\diff{\mathcal{L}}{c_1}=\frac{1}{3c_1}-\lambda=0,\qquad \diff{\mathcal{L}}{c_2}=\frac{2}{3c_2}-\lambda p_2=0.$$

The multiplier $\lambda$ must appear: the first-order conditions of Recipe #1 are those of the Lagrangian, and they set its derivatives equal to zero. Together with the budget constraint, they give $c_2=\dfrac{2c_1}{p_2}$ and $c_1^A=\dfrac{2+6p_2}{3}$, the demand given in Question 8.

Question 8

Question 8

Alice’s and Ben’s demands for consumption in state $s_1$, as functions of $p_2$, are

$$c_1^A(p_2)=\frac{2+6p_2}{3},\qquad c_1^B(p_2)=\frac{8+2p_2}{3}.$$

What is the equilibrium price $p_2$ in this model?

Choices: 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.25, 1.5, 1.75, 2, 2.25, 2.5, 2.75, 3.

Answer 8

Answer. $p_2=\frac52=2.5$.

In equilibrium the market for consumption in state $s_1$ clears: demand equals the total supply of Question 6,

$$c_1^A+c_1^B=\frac{2+6p_2}{3}+\frac{8+2p_2}{3}=\frac{10+8p_2}{3}=10\iff p_2=\frac{20}{8}=\frac52.$$

Every other choice leaves this market out of balance: demand exceeds $10$ when $p_2\gt\frac52$ and falls short of it when $p_2\lt\frac52$. The market for state $s_2$ then clears by Walras’ law. At $p_2=\frac52$, Alice consumes $\paren{\frac{17}{3},\frac{68}{15}}$ and Ben consumes $\paren{\frac{13}{3},\frac{52}{15}}$, which add up to the total endowment $(10,8)$.

Question 10

Question 10

There are no contingent markets, but there is a financial asset with returns $(r_1,r_2)=(5,-2)$ in states $s_1$ and $s_2$. Let $z$ be the amount invested in the asset. The spot budget constraints for Alice are (select all that apply):

  • $c_1+p_2c_2=2+6p_2$
  • $c_2=6+2z$
  • $c_1=2+5z$
  • $5c_1-2c_2=-2$
  • $c_1=6+5z$
  • $c_2=2-2z$
  • $c_1=2-5z$
  • $c_2=6-2z$
  • $c_1=8+5z$
  • $c_1-2c_2=-10$
  • $c_2=6+5z$
  • $c_1=2-2z$
Answer 10

Answer. $c_1=2+5z$ and $c_2=6-2z$.

With financial markets, there is one spot budget constraint per state: consumption in a state equals Alice’s endowment in that state plus the asset’s return times the amount invested. Spot prices are normalized to $1$.

  • $c_1+p_2c_2=2+6p_2$. Incorrect. This is the budget constraint with contingent markets, and there are none here.
  • $c_2=6+2z$. Incorrect. The return in state $s_2$ is $-2$, not $2$.
  • $c_1=2+5z$. Correct. Endowment $2$ plus return $5$ per dollar invested.
  • $5c_1-2c_2=-2$. Incorrect. It uses the returns as if they were prices of $c_1$ and $c_2$.
  • $c_1=6+5z$. Incorrect. Alice’s endowment in state $s_1$ is $2$; $6$ is her endowment in state $s_2$.
  • $c_2=2-2z$. Incorrect. Alice’s endowment in state $s_2$ is $6$, not $2$.
  • $c_1=2-5z$. Incorrect. The sign is flipped: the asset pays $+5$ per dollar in state $s_1$.
  • $c_2=6-2z$. Correct. Endowment $6$ plus return $-2$ per dollar invested.
  • $c_1=8+5z$. Incorrect. $8$ is Ben’s endowment in state $s_1$, not Alice’s.
  • $c_1-2c_2=-10$. Incorrect. It uses the return $-2$ as if it were the price of $c_2$.
  • $c_2=6+5z$. Incorrect. $5$ is the return in state $s_1$, not in state $s_2$.
  • $c_1=2-2z$. Incorrect. $-2$ is the return in state $s_2$, not in state $s_1$.

Every choice holds at the endowment, $z=0$, so checking the endowment alone does not single out the spot budget constraints. Eliminating $z$ from the two correct constraints gives $2c_1+5c_2=34$, that is, $c_1+\frac52c_2=2+6\cdot\frac52$: Alice’s budget constraint with contingent markets at the equilibrium price $p_2=\frac52$ of Question 8. Giving up $2$ in state $s_2$ buys $5$ in state $s_1$, so this one asset moves income between the two states and markets are complete.

Question 11

Question 11

Explain how, using the expected utility and the spot budget constraints, we can obtain Alice’s demand/supply for the financial asset, $z$. Please be brief.

Answer 11

Answer. Substitute the spot budget constraints of Question 10 into Alice’s expected utility, so that it becomes a function of $z$ alone, and maximize it with Recipe #0: set $\sddiff{U}{z}=0$ and solve for $z$.

Here,

$$U(z)=\tfrac13\ln(2+5z)+\tfrac23\ln(6-2z),$$ $$\ddiff{U}{z}=\frac{5}{3(2+5z)}-\frac{4}{3(6-2z)}=0\iff 5(6-2z)=4(2+5z)\iff z=\frac{11}{15}.$$

Alice buys $\frac{11}{15}$ of the asset and consumes $c_1=\frac{17}{3}$ and $c_2=\frac{68}{15}$, the same basket as in the contingent-markets equilibrium of Question 8.

Common mistakes:

  • This is Recipe #0, not Recipe #1: once the spot budget constraints are substituted, there is no constraint left, so there is no Lagrangian.
  • The order matters: first substitute $c_1$ and $c_2$, then take the derivative with respect to $z$.
  • There is no $p_1$ or $p_2$: those are contingent-market prices, and they appear neither in the spot budget constraints nor in the expected utility.
  • This is Alice’s individual choice, with the returns given. No market has to clear.

Question 12

Question 12

Two players choose $x$ and $y$, and their payoffs are $u_1(x,y)$ and $u_2(x,y)$. Select all the correct statements.

  • Every Nash equilibrium is inefficient.
  • $W=10\,u_1+u_2$ is a welfare function.
  • If some feasible $(x,y)$ makes player 1 better off than $(x_0,y_0)$, then $(x_0,y_0)$ is not efficient.
  • If the set of feasible utility profiles is convex and $(x_0,y_0)$ is efficient, then $(x_0,y_0)$ maximizes $W=a\,u_1+(1-a)\,u_2$ for some weight $0\lt a\lt 1$.
  • A Nash equilibrium must be efficient, since each player is maximizing their respective payoff.
  • At an efficient $(x_0,y_0)$, both players have the same payoff.
  • $(x_0,y_0)$ is efficient if there is no feasible $(x,y)$ that makes one player better off without making the other player worse off.
  • $W=10\,u_1-u_2$ is a welfare function.
  • If $x_0$ maximizes $u_1(x,y_0)$ with respect to $x$ and $y_0$ maximizes $u_2(x_0,y)$ with respect to $y$, then $(x_0,y_0)$ is efficient.
  • If $(x_0,y_0)$ is efficient, then it maximizes every welfare function.
Answer 12

Answer. Three statements are correct: $W=10\,u_1+u_2$ is a welfare function, Proposition Efficiency 2, and the definition of efficiency.

  • Every Nash equilibrium is inefficient. Incorrect. Some are efficient. If each player’s payoff depends only on their own choice, the Nash equilibrium gives both players their highest possible payoff, so it is efficient.
  • $W=10\,u_1+u_2$ is a welfare function. Correct. A welfare function is strictly increasing in every player’s payoff, and here $\sdiff{W}{u_1}=10\gt 0$ and $\sdiff{W}{u_2}=1\gt 0$.
  • If some feasible $(x,y)$ makes player 1 better off than $(x_0,y_0)$, then $(x_0,y_0)$ is not efficient. Incorrect. It forgets “without making the other player worse off”: at an efficient point, player 1 can still be made better off, but only by making player 2 worse off.
  • If the set of feasible utility profiles is convex and $(x_0,y_0)$ is efficient, then $(x_0,y_0)$ maximizes $W=a\,u_1+(1-a)\,u_2$ for some weight $0\lt a\lt 1$. Correct. This is Proposition Efficiency 2 with two players: the positive weights $w_1$ and $w_2$ can be rescaled to $a=\frac{w_1}{w_1+w_2}$ and $1-a$.
  • A Nash equilibrium must be efficient, since each player is maximizing their respective payoff. Incorrect. Each player maximizes their own payoff, taking the other’s choice as given and ignoring the effect on the other player. In the public good example, both players are better off if each contributes half her wealth than in the Nash equilibrium.
  • At an efficient $(x_0,y_0)$, both players have the same payoff. Incorrect. Efficiency is not equality. The maximizer of $W=10\,u_1+u_2$ is efficient (Proposition Efficiency 1), and it favors player 1.
  • $(x_0,y_0)$ is efficient if there is no feasible $(x,y)$ that makes one player better off without making the other player worse off. Correct. This is the definition of (Pareto) efficiency.
  • $W=10\,u_1-u_2$ is a welfare function. Incorrect. It decreases with player 2’s payoff: $\sdiff{W}{u_2}=-1\lt 0$.
  • If $x_0$ maximizes $u_1(x,y_0)$ with respect to $x$ and $y_0$ maximizes $u_2(x_0,y)$ with respect to $y$, then $(x_0,y_0)$ is efficient. Incorrect. This describes a Nash equilibrium, and a Nash equilibrium need not be efficient, as in the public good example.
  • If $(x_0,y_0)$ is efficient, then it maximizes every welfare function. Incorrect. $W=10\,u_1+u_2$ and $W=u_1+10\,u_2$ are both welfare functions, and they generally pick different efficient points. An efficient point maximizes some welfare function when the feasible set is convex, not every one.

Question 13

Now consider a different economy, a financial-markets economy with three states, $s_0$, $s_1$, and $s_2$, and three financial assets, $a$, $b$, and $c$, whose (gross) returns in each state are:

Consider the portfolio $\mathbf{w}=(-2,-2,4)$: you buy 4 units of asset $c$, and you sell 2 dollars’ worth of asset $b$ and 2 dollars’ worth of asset $a$.

Answer the following questions, labeling your answers 1) to 5) as below. Answers that are not labeled may lose points.

1) What is the return of the portfolio in state $s_0$?

2) What is the return of the portfolio in state $s_1$?

3) What is the return of the portfolio in state $s_2$?

4) Is this portfolio an arbitrage opportunity? Why or why not?

5) Is there an arbitrage opportunity in this economy? If yes, show a portfolio that implements the arbitrage strategy.

Answer 13

Answer. The return of a portfolio in a state is the sum, over the assets, of the amount held times the asset’s return in that state.

1) $(-2)(-1)+(-2)(-1)+4\cdot(-1)=2+2-4=0$.

2) $(-2)\cdot 8+(-2)\cdot 0+4\cdot 3=-16+12=-4$.

3) $(-2)\cdot 0+(-2)\cdot 8+4\cdot 3=-16+12=-4$.

4) No. The portfolio costs nothing in state $s_0$, but it loses 4 dollars in states $s_1$ and $s_2$: its returns $(0,-4,-4)$ are not all greater than or equal to zero, so it is not an arbitrage opportunity.

5) Yes. Reverse the portfolio: $-\mathbf{w}=(2,2,-4)$ buys 2 dollars’ worth of asset $a$ and 2 dollars’ worth of asset $b$, and sells 4 units of asset $c$. Its returns are $(0,4,4)$: nothing is paid in state $s_0$, and it earns 4 dollars in states $s_1$ and $s_2$. Any positive multiple works too; for example, $\paren{\frac12,\frac12,-1}$ returns $(0,1,1)$. Half a dollar each of assets $a$ and $b$ costs the same as one dollar of asset $c$ but pays $4$ instead of $3$ in both states.

A portfolio that costs money in state $s_0$ is not an arbitrage: for example, $(1,1,-1)$ returns $(-1,5,5)$.

Question 14

In the lecture notes (The Financial Structure, “Complete Markets”), we define complete markets as follows:

Definition: Given the financial structure (assets and returns), if for any two arbitrary states, we can always build a portfolio to transfer income between these two states while keeping the income in the other states constant, we say that markets are complete.

When markets are complete, the equilibrium allocation (equilibrium consumption baskets) in the financial economy and in the contingent goods economy are the same.

Read the article excerpt below and answer the following questions, labeling your answers 1) and 2).

1) In real life, are there contingent markets?

2) What is the relationship of the article quoted below to the financial markets we studied in our course?

Why sports bar owners are hedging against the teams they love

Andrew Rizzo’s family has owned the same piece of Chicago property next to Wrigley Field since 1946, when the family immigrated to the U.S. from Italy and opened Cubs Park Service Station. That business turned into parking for Bears and Chicago Cubs games until, in 2017, the family opened Rizzo’s Bar & Inn.

Like many bars in Wrigleyville, a significant portion of Rizzo’s revenue comes during baseball season, and a deep playoff run can seriously shift margins. Bars are packed, and because playoff tickets are more expensive, the bar often hosts wealthier clientele who spend more money. An early postseason exit, however, can mean unrealized revenue.

So as the Cubs squeaked their way into the postseason, Rizzo traded $7,500 on Kalshi that the Cubs would host two or fewer home playoff games. He hoped, both as a Chicagoan and businessowner, that he would be wrong. But when the Cubs never even made it to October, swept by the San Diego Padres in two away games during the Wild Card series, Rizzo’s bet paid out a few thousand dollars, softening the financial blow of a short postseason.

“Unfortunately in the past, like if the Cubs just had a short run or something and fell apart, you’re obviously really disappointed because you lost out on a ton of revenue,” Rizzo said.

This type of event-related risk has always existed in the hospitality industry, especially for sports bars and restaurants near stadiums. But as the use of prediction markets surges, especially in sports, some local businesses that rely on sports-related sales are using it as a form of insurance against potential lost revenue.

Rebecca Tauber, The New York Times, Oct. 30, 2026

Answer 14

Answer.

1) No. A contingent market trades contracts, signed today, for the delivery of goods and services in a particular state of the world: for example, an umbrella delivered tomorrow if it rains. We do not see such markets in real life, let alone a market for every good in every state. Prediction markets come close to replicating them, but they trade money, not goods.

2) The Kalshi contract is a financial asset: it pays money in one state (the Cubs host two or fewer home playoff games) and nothing in the other. In our notation, its gross returns are $(-1,r,0)$: one dollar paid before the playoffs, $r$ dollars in the “short postseason” state, and nothing in the “deep run” state.

Rizzo’s endowment, his bar’s revenue, is high when the Cubs go deep and low when they exit early. By buying the contract, he transfers income from the good state to the bad state: he gives up $7,500 of income in the good state, where the contract pays nothing, in exchange for a payout in the bad state. This is hedging: the consumption smoothing across states that a risk-averse agent does with financial assets in our model. The bet works as insurance against lost revenue.

It is not an arbitrage: the bet costs money up front, and it loses money in the good state.

Each contract like this one adds a way to move income between two states, so prediction markets make markets more complete, and complete financial markets reach the same allocation as contingent markets. Even so, a prediction market is a financial market, not a contingent market: when a state occurs, no good or service is delivered, only money.

Problem Set 06 Answer Key

Question 3

Does Dobson, Goddard & Stähler’s article have a theoretical model?

Answer. Yes. Dobson et al. use a contest model similar to the one in the lecture notes. The home team (team 1) and the away team (team 2) choose efforts $e_1$ and $e_2$:

$$r=p(0,0)=\frac{\beta_1+h}{\beta_1+\beta_2+h}\tag{1}$$ $$p(e_1,e_2)=\frac{e_1\,r}{e_1\,r+e_2\,(1-r)}\tag{2}$$ $$\pi_1=p\,U_1-c_1e_1,\qquad \pi_2=(1-p)\,U_2-c_2e_2\tag{3}$$

where $\beta_i$ is the quality of team $i$, $h$ is the home-field advantage, $r$ is the home team’s success probability when neither team exerts effort, $\pi_i$ is team $i$’s expected payoff, $U_i$ is team $i$’s payoff from winning, and $c_i$ is team $i$’s marginal cost of effort.

The main differences: the lecture notes assume $r=\tfrac12$ (no home-team advantage and teams of equal quality) and $c_1=c_2=1$ (the cost of effort is the same for both teams). Also, the lecture notes assume a quadratic cost of effort, while the article, like some other examples in the course, assumes a linear cost.

Problem Set 06 Answer Key

Question 4

What is the main prediction that Dobson, Goddard & Stähler’s article is trying to test?

Answer. The main prediction is the paper’s Result 2: when the two teams have the same cost of effort and the same payoff from a win, both exert the most effort in an evenly balanced game, that is, when $r=\tfrac12$ after allowing for home-field advantage. Asymmetries between the teams lower effort. The equilibrium efforts are

$$\begin{aligned} e_1^*&=\frac{r(1-r)\,c_2U_1^2U_2}{\left(r\,c_2U_1+(1-r)\,c_1U_2\right)^2}\\[4pt] e_2^*&=\frac{r(1-r)\,c_1U_1U_2^2}{\left(r\,c_2U_1+(1-r)\,c_1U_2\right)^2} \end{aligned}\tag{4}$$

With $c_1=c_2=c$ and $U_1=U_2=U$, both reduce to $e^*=r(1-r)\,U/c$, which is largest at $r=\tfrac12$. This is why the empirical model includes $\text{UNCERT}=\text{HPROB}\cdot(1-\text{HPROB})$, where HPROB is their measure of $r$: the prediction is a positive coefficient on UNCERT.

The paper also tests Result 1: a team exerts more effort when its own payoff from a win, $U_i$, is larger. A game that still matters for a team’s championship, European qualification, promotion or relegation chances has a larger $U_i$. The empirical model captures this with the dummies HSIG (home team) and ASIG (away team).

The coefficients on UNCERT are positive in every equation, as Result 2 predicts. Result 1 holds for away teams, whose fouls and cards rise when the game matters to them. The home team’s effort does not respond significantly to its own stakes.

How is “effort” measured in Dobson, Goddard & Stähler’s article?
