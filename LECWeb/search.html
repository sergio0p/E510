Search

Search problems are dynamic problems with an infinite horizon where the agent has to decide to:

  • "Should I stay or should I go?"—The Clash (1981).
  • Should I accept the current job offer or keep searching for better jobs?
  • Should I settle down or keep searching for a life partner?
  • Should I buy plane tickets now or keep searching for better deals?
  • Should I enter this market now or delay entry?
  • Should I exit this market now or delay exit?
  • Should I stop tuning and deploy this model or keep searching for better parameters?
  • Should I attack now or defend and wait for an opportunity of defeating the enemy?—Sun Tzu, The Art of War

A Model

  • At each period $t$, the agent chooses $a_t \in \{\text{stop}, \text{continue}\}$.
  • There is a set of states $S$ and the probability of the state in period $t$ being equal to $x_t$ given past state realizations satisfies: $$\Pr(s_t = x_t \mid s_{t-1} = x_{t-1}, s_{t-2} = x_{t-2}, \ldots) = \Pr(s_t = x_t \mid s_{t-1} = x_{t-1}) = q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})$$ Note: "states" here are not the same as "states" in the previous sections.
  • In each period $t$, provided the agent did not stop yet, the agent gets utility $u(s_t, a_t)$—it depends only on the state and the action, not on calendar time. If the agent chooses to stop, $a_t = \text{stop}$, the agent receives $u(s_t, \text{stop})$ at period $t$ and zero in future periods.
  • The agent maximizes discounted expected utility: $$U(s_0, \mathbf{a}(\cdot)) = \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \delta^t \, u(s_t, a_t)\right]$$

The Value Function

Notice that the value function will depend only on the current state but NOT on the calendar time. The value function must satisfy the recursive equation:

$$V(s_t) = \max\left\{ u(s_t, \text{stop}), \; u(s_t, \text{continue}) + \delta \sum_{s_{t+1} \in S} V(s_{t+1}) \cdot q(s_{t+1}|s_t) \right\}$$

A Tree Cutting Problem

Fudenberg & Tirole, Game Theory (1991)

A landowner has a tree whose timber value $v(t)$ grows with age $t$, but at a diminishing rate ($v(0) = 0$, $v' \gt 0$, $v'' \lt 0$). At each period $t$, the owner decides to cut the tree (stop) or to wait (continue):

  • If the owner cuts: $u(t, \text{stop}) = v(t)$
  • If the owner waits: $u(t, \text{continue}) = 0$

The transition is deterministic: state $t$ transitions to $t + 1$ with certainty.

The value function satisfies:

$$V(t) = \max\left\{ v(t), \; \delta \cdot V(t+1) \right\}$$

A Tree Cutting Problem

Continuation

If $t^\ast$ is the optimal stopping time:

Cutting earlier is not profitable: at $t^\ast - 1$, waiting yields more than cutting:

At period $t^\ast - 1$, $$v(t^\ast - 1) \leq \delta \cdot v(t^\ast)$$

In particular, cutting the tree at period $t=0$ is never optimal, $v(0) = 0 \lt \delta \cdot v(1)$.

Cutting later is not profitable: cutting at $t^\ast$ yields more than waiting:

At period $t^\ast$, $$v(t^\ast) \geq \delta \cdot v(t^\ast + 1)$$

Online Shopping

An Example

A consumer is searching to buy one unit of a good online. Let $p_t$ denote the price at time $t$.

  • If the consumer buys (stops): $u(p_t, \text{stop}) = v - p_t$
  • If the consumer keeps searching (continues): $u(p_t, \text{continue}) = 0$

The price increases by a factor of $2$ or decreases by a factor of $1/2$, each with equal probability:

$$q(2 p \mid p) = q(p/2 \mid p) = \tfrac{1}{2}$$

Notice that any price is of the form $p_t = 2^z \cdot p_0$ where $z$ is an integer. Thus, for simplicity, we use the set of integers $\mathbb{Z}$ as the set of states.

Online Shopping

An Example (continuation)

It can be shown that any optimal strategy will be a threshold strategy. There is a reservation price $p_0 2^z$ such that the consumer buys only if the current price is at or below the reservation price $p_t \leq p_0 2^z$. Using the "state" notation:

$$a^z(s_t) = \begin{cases} \text{stop} & \text{if } s_t \leq z \\ \text{continue} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

Our goal is then to find the value of $z$ such that the recursive equation $\textcolor{#c17900}{(\text{R})}$ is satisfied.

Let $V^z(s_t) = U(s_t, a^z(\cdot))$ be the value of state $s_t$ if the agent follows the strategy $a^z$. Notice that $V^z$ might not be the value function. The function $V^z$ satisfies some recursive equation but it might not satisfy the recursive equation $\textcolor{#c17900}{(\text{R})}$.

$$V^z(s) = \begin{cases} v - p_0 2^s & \text{if } s \leq z \\ \frac{\delta}{2} \cdot \left( v - p_0 \cdot 2^z + V^z(s+1) \right) & \text{if } s = z+1 \\ \frac{\delta}{2} \cdot \left( V^z(s-1) + V^z(s+1) \right) & \text{if } s \gt z+1 \end{cases} \quad (\text{Z})$$

$$V^z(s) = \begin{cases} v - p_0 2^s & \text{if } s \leq z \\ (v - p_0 2^z) \cdot r^{s-z} & \text{if } s \gt z \end{cases}$$

where $0 \lt r = \dfrac{1 - \sqrt{1 - \delta^2}}{\delta} \lt 1$.

Let $z^\ast$ be the optimal $z$, then it must satisfy the One-shot Deviation Principle we shall see next.

One-Shot Deviation Principle

Definition: One-Shot Deviation
Let $a = (a_t)$ be some search policy. The alternative policy $\tilde{a} = (\tilde{a}_t)$ is a one-shot deviation at period $t$ and state $s_t$ if $\tilde{a}_t(s_t) \neq a_t(s_t)$ but $\tilde{a}_k(s) = a_k(s)$ for all future periods $k \gt t$ and all states $s$.
Definition: Profitable One-Shot Deviation
A one-shot deviation $\tilde{a}$ from policy $a$ at period $t$ and state $s_t$ is profitable if it yields a strictly higher expected discounted utility than $a$ at $s_t$ where $\tilde{a}_t(s_t) \neq a_t(s_t)$.
The One-Shot Deviation Principle
Suppose $0 \lt \delta \lt 1$ and the utility $u$ is bounded. A policy $a^\ast = (a_t^\ast)$ is optimal if and only if there is no alternative policy $\tilde{a}$ that is a profitable one-shot deviation.

One-Shot Deviation Principle and The Value Function

In states where the optimal policy prescribes stopping, the one-shot deviation principle requires that deviating to continue is not profitable:

$$V(s_t) = u(s_t, \text{stop}) \geq u(s_t, \text{continue}) + \delta \sum_{s_{t+1} \in S} V(s_{t+1}) \cdot q(s_{t+1}|s_t)$$

In states where the optimal policy prescribes continuing, the one-shot deviation principle requires that deviating to stop is not profitable:

$$V(s_t) = u(s_t, \text{continue}) + \delta \sum_{s_{t+1} \in S} V(s_{t+1}) \cdot q(s_{t+1}|s_t) \geq u(s_t, \text{stop})$$

Why can we use the value function to obtain the lifetime payoff of a one-shot deviation? Notice that after the deviation, the agent resumes following the optimal path and therefore the agent's future (continuation) utility can be expressed by means of the value function.

The One-Shot Deviation Principle in Action

If the value of $z$ is optimal, the One-Shot Deviation Principle requires two conditions at the boundary:

At $s = z$ (agent stops): deviating to continue should not be profitable:

$$v - p_02^{z} \geq \frac{\delta}{2}\left(V^{z}(z - 1) + V^{z}(z + 1)\right)$$

At $s = z + 1$ (agent continues): deviating to stop should not be profitable:

$$\frac{\delta}{2}\left(V^{z}(z) + V^{z}(z + 2)\right) \geq v - p_02^{z+1}$$

Define $f(z) = v - p_0 2^z$, $h(z) = \frac{\delta}{2}\left(V^z(z-1) + V^z(z+1)\right)$ and $g(z) = \frac{\delta}{2}\left(V^z(z) + V^z(z+2)\right)$. For the optimal $z$:

$$f(z) \geq h(z) \geq g(z) \geq f(z+1)$$

Here $u(p_t, \text{stop}) = v - p_t$ is not bounded below, but the principle still applies: utility is bounded above by $v$, and every $V^z$ is bounded.

Job Search

An Example

An agent is searching for jobs. For simplicity, we assume that with equal probability the agent receives:

  • No offers,
  • A low job offer $w=10$, or
  • A high job offer $w=20$.

The agent discounts time at the rate $0 \lt \delta \lt 1$. Also, for simplicity, we assume that if the agent accepts a job offer, the agent receives the corresponding wage in all future periods. We are ruling out that the agent may be fired with some positive probability in the future or that the agent may decide to quit the job in the future.

Job Search

An Example

Here, stop means accepting the job offer and continue means rejecting the job offer. There are three states: $N$ (no offers), $L$ (low offer) and $H$ (high offer). Also, choosing stop in state $N$ means that the agent decided to quit the workforce. The utility of stopping is then:

$$u(N,\text{stop})=0 \lt u(L,\text{stop})=\frac{10}{1-\delta} \lt u(H,\text{stop})=\frac{20}{1-\delta}$$

Since we assume that there are no opportunity costs of searching, the optimal strategy is going to be either to accept any job offer, $\sigma_{LH}$, or to accept only the high wage job offer, $\sigma_{H}$.

Let's analyze the $\sigma_H$ strategy:

$$\begin{aligned} V^{\sigma_H}(N) &= 0 + \delta\left[\tfrac{1}{3}V^{\sigma_H}(N) + \tfrac{1}{3}V^{\sigma_H}(L) + \tfrac{1}{3}V^{\sigma_H}(H)\right]\\[6pt] V^{\sigma_H}(L) &= 0 + \delta\left[\tfrac{1}{3}V^{\sigma_H}(N) + \tfrac{1}{3}V^{\sigma_H}(L) + \tfrac{1}{3}V^{\sigma_H}(H)\right]\\[6pt] V^{\sigma_H}(H) &= \frac{20}{1-\delta} \end{aligned}$$

Solving this linear system of equations we obtain:

$$V^{\sigma_H}(N) = V^{\sigma_H}(L) = \frac{20\delta}{(1-\delta)(3-2\delta)} \quad\text{and}\quad V^{\sigma_H}(H) = \frac{20}{1-\delta}$$

We only need to check for one-shot deviations at state $L$ (deviating to stop):

$$V^{\sigma_H}(L) \geq u(L,\text{stop}) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{20\delta}{(1-\delta)(3-2\delta)} \geq \frac{10}{1-\delta} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \delta \geq \tfrac{3}{4}$$

In sum, more patient agents ($\delta \geq \tfrac{3}{4}$) will only accept the high wage job offer, while more impatient agents ($\delta \lt \tfrac{3}{4}$) will accept any job offer. The expected unemployment duration is longer for patient agents ($2$ periods) than for impatient ones ($\tfrac{1}{2}$ period).

A Queue Problem

A Learning-by-Doing Example

Setup: You are standing in line to buy a ticket for a game. The ticket is worth $v$ to you and its price is $p$, where $v-p\gt 0$. The length of the line, $\ell$, is the number of people waiting in front of you, a nonnegative integer.

Time is discrete, $t=0,1,2,\ldots$, and there is no terminal date: the horizon is infinite. At the beginning of each period, you choose between staying in line until the next period ($\text{continue}$) and leaving the line for good ($\text{stop}$). If you continue, you incur a cost $c\gt 0$, and in the next period the line advances with probability $q$, where $0\lt q\lt 1$, so its length becomes $\ell-1$; with probability $1-q$ it stays the same. When $\ell=0$, it is your turn: you incur no waiting cost and receive $v-p$. You discount future payoffs and costs by the factor $\delta$, where $0\lt\delta\lt 1$.

Question 1

Which state variable(s) should we use for this problem?

  1. The time period, $t$.
  2. The length of the line, $\ell$.
  3. Both, $(t,\ell)$.
Answer 1

Answer. The length of the line, $\ell$. The horizon is infinite, and the payoffs, the waiting cost, the probability that the line advances, and the discount factor do not change over time. A line of length $\ell$ therefore poses the same problem in every period, and the time period $t$ adds nothing.

Question 2

For any strategy you might follow, what is the value function at the state $\ell=0$, $V(0)$?

Answer 2

Answer. $V(0)=v-p$. When $\ell=0$ it is your turn: you buy the ticket, incur no waiting cost, and the problem ends. No choice is left to make, so every strategy gives the same value.

Question 3

Suppose you always choose to continue. Write $V(1)$ in terms of $V(0)$, $c$, $\delta$, and $q$.

Answer 3

Answer. If you continue, you pay $c$ today. Next period the line advances with probability $q$, which takes you to the state $0$; with probability $1-q$ it stays at length $1$. Discounting next period’s value by $\delta$:

$$V(1)=-c+\delta\paren{q\,V(0)+(1-q)\,V(1)}.$$

Solving for $V(1)$:

$$V(1)=\frac{\delta q\,V(0)-c}{1-\delta(1-q)}.$$
Question 4

Suppose you always choose to continue. For a generic length $\ell\ge 1$, write $V(\ell)$ in terms of $V(\ell-1)$, $c$, $\delta$, and $q$.

Answer 4

Answer. If you continue, you pay $c$ today. Next period the line advances with probability $q$, which takes you to the state $\ell-1$; with probability $1-q$ it stays at length $\ell$. Discounting next period’s value by $\delta$:

$$V(\ell)=-c+\delta\paren{q\,V(\ell-1)+(1-q)\,V(\ell)}.$$

Solving for $V(\ell)$:

$$V(\ell)=\frac{\delta q\,V(\ell-1)-c}{1-\delta(1-q)}.$$
Question 5

Suppose that, for any $\ell\ge 1$, $V(\ell)=A\,V(\ell-1)+B$, where $A$ and $B$ are constants. Write $V(\ell)$ as a function of $V(\ell-2)$, $A$, and $B$.

Answer 5

Answer. For any $\ell\ge 2$, apply the relation twice, first at $\ell$ and then at $\ell-1$:

$$V(\ell)=A\,V(\ell-1)+B=A\paren{A\,V(\ell-2)+B}+B=A^2\,V(\ell-2)+(1+A)\,B.$$
Question 6

Use the answers to the previous questions to express $V(\ell)$ as a function of $V(\ell-3)$, $A$, and $B$.

Answer 6

Answer. For any $\ell\ge 3$, start from Answer 5 and apply the relation once more, at $\ell-2$:

$$\begin{align*} V(\ell)&=A^2\,V(\ell-2)+(1+A)\,B=A^2\paren{A\,V(\ell-3)+B}+(1+A)\,B\\ &=A^3\,V(\ell-3)+(1+A+A^2)\,B. \end{align*}$$
Question 7

Guess: how would we write $V(\ell)$ as a function of $V(0)$, $A$, and $B$ only?

Answer 7

Answer. Carry the pattern from Answers 5 and 6 all the way down to $V(0)$:

$$V(\ell)=A^\ell\,V(0)+\paren{1+A+\cdots+A^{\ell-1}}B.$$
Question 8

Using the recursive method we first learned in the last chapter (for series, not value functions), compute a simpler expression for $1+A+\cdots+A^{\ell-1}$.

Answer 8

Answer. Let $S=1+A+A^2+\cdots+A^{\ell-1}$.

  • Factor out $A$: $S=1+A\paren{1+A+\cdots+A^{\ell-2}}$.
  • The expression in parentheses is $S$ without its last term, $A^{\ell-1}$: $S=1+A\paren{S-A^{\ell-1}}$.
  • This is a recursive equation: $S$ is defined in terms of $S$.
  • Solve: $S-AS=1-A^\ell$, that is, $(1-A)\,S=1-A^\ell$.
  • Solution: $S=\dfrac{1-A^\ell}{1-A}$.
Question 9

Suppose the line has length $\ell$. When would you prefer to stop, that is, to leave the line? Answer with an inequality that involves only $\ell$, $V(0)$, $A$, and $B$: “I would stop when …”

Answer 9

Answer. Stopping gives you $0$: you leave with nothing and pay nothing more. Continuing until your turn is worth $V(\ell)$, from Answers 7 and 8. I would stop when

$$A^\ell\,V(0)+\frac{1-A^\ell}{1-A}\,B\lt 0.$$
Question 10

In terms of the model’s exogenous variables—$v$, $p$, $c$, $\delta$, and $q$—what are $A$ and $B$ in our setting? What is the sign of $A$? What is the sign of $B$?

Answer 10

Answer. The equation in Answer 4 already has the form $V(\ell)=A\,V(\ell-1)+B$, with

$$A=\frac{\delta q}{1-\delta(1-q)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad B=-\frac{c}{1-\delta(1-q)}.$$

The denominator $1-\delta(1-q)$ is positive, because $\delta\lt 1$ and $1-q\lt 1$. Hence $A\gt 0$ and, since $c\gt 0$, $B\lt 0$. The values $v$ and $p$ appear in neither $A$ nor $B$; they enter only through $V(0)=v-p$.

Question 11

Is $A\gt 1$, $A=1$, or $A\lt 1$? Justify your answer.

Answer 11

Answer. $A\lt 1$. The denominator of $A$ is positive, so multiplying both sides by it keeps the inequality:

$$A\lt 1\iff\delta q\lt 1-\delta(1-q)\iff\delta q\lt 1-\delta+\delta q\iff\delta\lt 1,$$

and $\delta\lt 1$ by assumption.

Question 12

What happens to the expression

$$V(\ell)=A^\ell\,V(0)+\frac{1-A^\ell}{1-A}\,B$$

as $\ell$ increases? Does it increase, decrease, or stay constant? Justify your answer.

Answer 12

Answer. It decreases. Take the two terms one at a time.

  • First term, $A^\ell\,V(0)$: since $0\lt A\lt 1$ (Answers 10 and 11), $A^\ell$ falls as $\ell$ grows. Since $V(0)=v-p\gt 0$, the term falls.
  • Second term: by Answer 8, $\dfrac{1-A^\ell}{1-A}=1+A+\cdots+A^{\ell-1}$, which gains the positive term $A^\ell$ each time $\ell$ goes up by one, so it rises. Since $B\lt 0$, the term falls.

Both terms fall, so $V(\ell)$ decreases as $\ell$ increases. A longer line means more periods of waiting costs and a ticket further in the future.

Question 13

Show that $V(\ell)$ becomes negative when $\ell$ is sufficiently large. Hint: what is the limit of $V(\ell)$ as $\ell\to\infty$?

Answer 13

Answer. Since $0\lt A\lt 1$, $A^\ell\to 0$ as $\ell\to\infty$, so

$$\lim_{\ell\to\infty}V(\ell)=\frac{B}{1-A}.$$

By Answer 10, $1-A=\dfrac{1-\delta}{1-\delta(1-q)}$, so

$$\frac{B}{1-A}=-\frac{c}{1-\delta}\lt 0.$$

Hence $V(\ell)$ gets arbitrarily close to a negative number, so $V(\ell)\lt 0$ once $\ell$ is large enough. Since $V(\ell)$ decreases (Answer 12), it stays negative for every longer line. The limit $-c/(1-\delta)$ is the value of paying $c$ in every period forever: an endless line means waiting costs forever and never a ticket.

Question 14

Remember that $V(\ell)$ is the value of the strategy that never leaves the line. In your own words, describe the optimal strategy for this problem.

Answer 14

Answer. Stay only if the line is short enough. By Answers 12 and 13, $V(\ell)$ starts at $V(0)=v-p\gt 0$, decreases as $\ell$ grows, and eventually turns negative. Let $\ell^\ast$ be the largest length with $V(\ell^\ast)\ge 0$. The optimal strategy uses $\ell^\ast$ as a cutoff:

  • If $\ell\le\ell^\ast$: continue, and stay in line until your turn.
  • If $\ell\gt\ell^\ast$: stop, and leave the line right away.

Once you decide to stay, the line only gets shorter or stays the same, so its length never rises above $\ell^\ast$. You remain in line until it is your turn.

Question 15

Let $V^\ast(\ell)$ be the value function: your discounted utility when you follow the optimal strategy from a line of length $\ell$. So far, $V(\ell)$ has been the utility of never leaving the line, and we have loosely called it a “value” as well. What recursive equation must $V^\ast$ satisfy?

Answer 15

Answer. At $\ell=0$ there is no choice to make: $V^\ast(0)=v-p$. For $\ell\ge 1$, the recursive equation (R) with $u(\ell,\text{stop})=0$ and $u(\ell,\text{continue})=-c$ gives

$$V^\ast(\ell)=\max\left\{0,\;-c+\delta\paren{q\,V^\ast(\ell-1)+(1-q)\,V^\ast(\ell)}\right\}.$$

The first entry is the value of stopping now. The second is the value of continuing for one period and then following the optimal strategy.

A Queue Problem

Learning How Fast the Line Moves

Setup: You are standing in line to buy a ticket for a game. The ticket is worth $v$ to you and its price is $p$, where $v-p\gt 0$. The length of the line, $\ell$, is the number of people waiting in front of you, a nonnegative integer.

Time is discrete, $t=0,1,2,\ldots$, and there is no terminal date: the horizon is infinite. At the beginning of each period, you choose between staying in line until the next period ($\text{continue}$) and leaving the line for good ($\text{stop}$). If you continue, you incur a cost $c\gt 0$, and in the next period the line advances with probability $q$, so its length becomes $\ell-1$; with probability $1-q$ it stays the same. When $\ell=0$, it is your turn: you incur no waiting cost and receive $v-p$. You discount future payoffs and costs by the factor $\delta$, where $0\lt\delta\lt 1$.

Unlike in the previous example, you do not know how fast the line moves: either $q=\tfrac34$ (the line moves fast) or $q=\tfrac14$ (the line moves slowly). At time $t=0$, your belief that the line moves fast is $\beta_0$, that is, $\Pr(q=\tfrac34)=\beta_0$ and $\Pr(q=\tfrac14)=1-\beta_0$. At time $t$, if you are still in line, $\beta_t$ denotes your updated belief that the line moves fast.

Click on a question to reveal its answer.

Question 1

Suppose you stay in line in period $t=0$. What is $\beta_1$ if the line moves? What is $\beta_1$ if it does not move?

Answer 1

Answer. Use Bayes’ rule. If the line moves:

$$\beta_1=\frac{\tfrac34\beta_0}{\tfrac34\beta_0+\tfrac14(1-\beta_0)}=\frac{3\beta_0}{1+2\beta_0}.$$

If it does not move:

$$\beta_1=\frac{\tfrac14\beta_0}{\tfrac14\beta_0+\tfrac34(1-\beta_0)}=\frac{\beta_0}{3-2\beta_0}.$$

A line that moves makes you more confident that it is fast: $\beta_1\gt\beta_0$ whenever $0\lt\beta_0\lt 1$. A line that stalls makes you less confident.

Question 2

Which state variable(s) should we use for this problem?

  1. The time period, $t$.
  2. The length of the line, $\ell$.
  3. Your current belief that the line moves fast, $\beta$.
  4. The time period and your belief, $(t,\beta)$.
  5. The length of the line and your belief, $(\ell,\beta)$.
  6. All three, $(t,\ell,\beta)$.
Answer 2

Answer. The length of the line and your belief, $(\ell,\beta)$. The length matters as before. Now your belief matters too, because it sets the probability that the line moves next period, $\beta\cdot\tfrac34+(1-\beta)\cdot\tfrac14$. Given $\ell$ and $\beta$, the problem looks the same whatever the date, so the time period $t$ adds nothing.

The pair $(t,\ell)$ would also work. The initial length $\ell_0$ and the initial belief $\beta_0$ are fixed, and by period $t$ the line has moved $\ell_0-\ell$ times and stalled $t-(\ell_0-\ell)$ times, so $\beta_t$ can be computed from $t$ and $\ell$. The drawback is that a value function of $(t,\ell)$ changes with the date, while a value function of $(\ell,\beta)$ does not.

Question 3

Let $V^\ast(\ell,\beta)$ be the value function: your discounted utility when you follow the optimal strategy from a line of length $\ell$ with belief $\beta$. What recursive equation must $V^\ast$ satisfy?

Answer 3

Answer. At $\ell=0$ there is no choice to make: $V^\ast(0,\beta)=v-p$. For $\ell\ge 1$, the line moves next period with probability

$$\pi(\beta)=\beta\cdot\tfrac34+(1-\beta)\cdot\tfrac14=\frac{1+2\beta}{4},$$

and, as in Answer 1, your belief becomes $\beta^+=\dfrac{3\beta}{1+2\beta}$ if it moves and $\beta^-=\dfrac{\beta}{3-2\beta}$ if it does not. The recursive equation (R) with $u(\ell,\beta,\text{stop})=0$ and $u(\ell,\beta,\text{continue})=-c$ gives

$$V^\ast(\ell,\beta)=\max\left\{0,\;-c+\delta\paren{\pi(\beta)\,V^\ast(\ell-1,\beta^+)+(1-\pi(\beta))\,V^\ast(\ell,\beta^-)}\right\}.$$

The first entry is the value of stopping now. The second is the value of continuing for one period and then following the optimal strategy.

We will not solve this example. One way to solve it is to guess that the optimal strategy follows a cutoff rule: there is a cutoff length $\ell^\ast(\beta)$ such that you continue when $\ell\le\ell^\ast(\beta)$ and stop when $\ell\gt\ell^\ast(\beta)$, where $\ell^\ast(\beta)$ is increasing in $\beta$. The more confident you are that the line moves fast, the longer the line you are willing to wait in.
