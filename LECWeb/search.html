Search

Search problems are dynamic problems with an infinite horizon where the agent has to decide to:

  • "Should I stay or should I go?"—The Clash (1981).
  • Should I accept the current job offer or keep searching for better jobs?
  • Should I settle down or keep searching for a life partner?
  • Should I buy plane tickets now or keep searching for better deals?
  • Should I enter this market now or delay entry?
  • Should I exit this market now or delay exit?
  • Should I stop tuning and deploy this model or keep searching for better parameters?
  • Should I attack now or defend and wait for an opportunity of defeating the enemy?—Sun Tzu, The Art of War

A Model

  • At each period $t$, the agent chooses $a_t =$ stop $s$ or continue $c$.
  • There is a set of states $S$ and the probability of the state in period $t$ being equal to $x_t$ given past state realizations satisfies: $$\Pr(s_t = x_t \mid s_{t-1} = x_{t-1}, s_{t-2} = x_{t-2}, \ldots) = \Pr(s_t = x_t \mid s_{t-1} = x_{t-1}) = q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})$$ Note: "states" here are not the same as "states" in the previous sections.
  • In each period $t$, provided the agent did not stop yet, the agent gets utility $u(s_t, a_t)$—it depends only on the state and the action, not on calendar time. If the agent chooses to stop, $a_t = s$, the agent receives $u(s_t, s)$ at period $t$ and zero in future periods.
  • The agent maximizes discounted expected utility: $$U(s_t, \mathbf{a}(\cdot)) = \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \delta^t \, u(s_t, a_t)\right]$$

The Value Function

Notice that the value function will depend only on the current state but NOT on the calendar time. The value function must satisfy the recursive equation:

$$V(s_t) = \max\left\{ u(s_t, s), \; u(s_t, c) + \delta \sum_{s_{t+1} \in S} V(s_{t+1}) \cdot q(s_{t+1}|s_t) \right\}$$

A Tree Cutting Problem

Fudenberg & Tirole, Game Theory (1991)

A landowner has a tree whose timber value $v(t)$ grows with age $t$, but at a diminishing rate ($v(0) = 0$, $v' \gt 0$, $v'' \lt 0$). At each period $t$, the owner decides to cut the tree ($s$) or to wait ($w$):

  • If the owner cuts: $u(t, s) = v(t)$
  • If the owner waits: $u(t, w) = 0$

The transition is deterministic: state $t$ transitions to $t + 1$ with certainty.

The value function satisfies:

$$V(t) = \max\left\{ v(t), \; \delta \cdot V(t+1) \right\}$$

A Tree Cutting Problem

Continuation

If $t^\ast$ is the optimal stopping time:

Cutting earlier is not profitable: at $t^\ast - 1$, waiting yields more than cutting:

At period $t^\ast - 1$, $$v(t^\ast - 1) \leq \delta \cdot v(t^\ast)$$

In particular, cutting the tree at period $t=0$ is never optimal, $v(0) = 0 \lt \delta \cdot v(1)$.

Cutting later is not profitable: cutting at $t^\ast$ yields more than waiting:

At period $t^\ast$, $$v(t^\ast) \geq \delta \cdot v(t^\ast + 1)$$

Online Shopping

An Example

A consumer is searching to buy one unit of a good online. Let $p_t$ denote the price at time $t$.

  • If the consumer buys (stops): $u(p_t, s) = v - p_t$
  • If the consumer keeps searching (continues): $u(p_t, c) = 0$

The price increases by a factor of $2$ or decreases by a factor of $1/2$, each with equal probability:

$$q(2 p \mid p) = q(p/2 \mid p) = \tfrac{1}{2}$$

Notice that any price is of the form $p_t = 2^z \cdot p_0$ where $z$ is an integer. Thus, for simplicity, we use the set of integers $\mathbb{Z}$ as the set of states.

Online Shopping

An Example (continuation)

It can be shown that any optimal strategy will be a threshold strategy. There is a reservation price $p_0 2^z$ such that the consumer buys only if the current price is at or below the reservation price $p_t \leq p_0 2^z$. Using the "state" notation:

$$a^z(s_t) = \begin{cases} s & \text{if } s_t \leq z \\ c & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

Our goal is then to find the value of $z$ such that the recursive equation $\textcolor{#c17900}{(\text{R})}$ is satisfied.

Let $V^z(s_t) = U(s_t, a^z(\cdot))$ be the value of state $s_t$ if the agent follows the strategy $a^z$. Notice that $V^z$ might not be the value function. The function $V^z$ satisfies some recursive equation but it might not satisfy the recursive equation $\textcolor{#c17900}{(\text{R})}$.

$$V^z(s) = \begin{cases} v - p_0 2^s & \text{if } s \leq z \\ \frac{\delta}{2} \cdot \left( v - p_0 \cdot 2^z + V^z(s+1) \right) & \text{if } s = z+1 \\ \frac{\delta}{2} \cdot \left( V^z(s-1) + V^z(s+1) \right) & \text{if } s \gt z+1 \end{cases} \quad (\text{Z})$$

An Example (solution)

$$V^z(s) = \begin{cases} v - p_0 2^s & \text{if } s \leq z \\ (v - p_0 2^z) \cdot r^{s-z} & \text{if } s \gt z \end{cases}$$

where $0 \lt r = \dfrac{1 - \sqrt{1 - \delta^2}}{\delta} \leq 1$.

We can use Mathematica RSolve to solve the recursive equation $(\text{Z})$.

Let $z^\ast$ be the optimal $z$, then it must satisfy the One-shot Deviation Principle we shall see next.

One-Shot Deviation Principle

Definition: One-Shot Deviation
Let $a = (a_t)$ be some search policy. The alternative policy $\tilde{a} = (\tilde{a}_t)$ is a one-shot deviation at period $t$ and state $s_t$ if $\tilde{a}_t(s_t) \neq a_t(s_t)$ but $\tilde{a}_k(s) = a_k(s)$ for all future periods $k \gt t$ and all states $s$.
Definition: Profitable One-Shot Deviation
A one-shot deviation $\tilde{a}$ from policy $a$ at period $t$ and state $s_t$ is profitable if it yields a strictly higher expected discounted utility than $a$ at $s_t$ where $\tilde{a}_t(s_t) \neq a_t(s_t)$.
The One-Shot Deviation Principle
A policy $a^\ast = (a_t^\ast)$ is optimal if and only if there is no alternative policy $\tilde{a}$ that is a profitable one-shot deviation.

One-Shot Deviation Principle and The Value Function

In states where the optimal policy prescribes stopping, the one-shot deviation principle requires that deviating to continue is not profitable:

$$V(s_t) = u(s_t, s) \geq u(s_t, c) + \delta \sum_{s_{t+1} \in S} V(s_{t+1}) \cdot q(s_{t+1}|s_t)$$

In states where the optimal policy prescribes continuing, the one-shot deviation principle requires that deviating to stop is not profitable:

$$V(s_t) = u(s_t, c) + \delta \sum_{s_{t+1} \in S} V(s_{t+1}) \cdot q(s_{t+1}|s_t) \geq u(s_t, s)$$

Why can we use the value function to obtain the lifetime payoff of a one-shot deviation? Notice that after the deviation, the agent resumes following the optimal path and therefore the agent's future (continuation) utility can be expressed by means of the value function.

The One-Shot Deviation Principle in Action

If the value of $z$ is optimal, the One-Shot Deviation Principle requires two conditions at the boundary:

At $s = z$ (agent stops): deviating to continue should not be profitable:

$$v - p_02^{z} \geq \frac{\delta}{2}\left(V^{z}(z - 1) + V^{z}(z + 1)\right)$$

At $s = z + 1$ (agent continues): deviating to stop should not be profitable:

$$\frac{\delta}{2}\left(V^{z}(z) + V^{z}(z + 2)\right) \geq v - p_02^{z+1}$$

Define $f(z) = v - p_0 2^z$ and $g(z) = \frac{\delta}{2}\left(V^z(z) + V^z(z+2)\right)$, for the optimal $z$:

$$f(z) \geq g(z-1) \geq g(z) \geq f(z+1)$$

Job Search

An Example

An agent is searching for jobs. For simplicity, we assume that with equal probability the agent receives:

  • No offers,
  • A low job offer $w=10$, or
  • A high job offer $w=20$.

The agent discounts time at the rate $0 \lt \delta \lt 1$. Also, for simplicity, we assume that if the agent accepts a job offer, the agent receives the corresponding wage in all future periods. We are ruling out that the agent may be fired with some positive probability in the future or that the agent may decide to quit the job in the future.

Job Search

An Example

Here, $s$ means accepting the job offer and $c$ means rejecting the job offer. There are three states: $N$ (no offers), $L$ (low offer) and $H$ (high offer). Also, choosing $s$ (stop searching) in state $N$ means that the agent decided to quit the workforce. The utility of stopping is then:

$$u(N,s)=0 \lt u(L,s)=\frac{10}{1-\delta} \lt u(H,s)=\frac{20}{1-\delta}$$

Since we assume that there are no opportunity costs of searching, the optimal strategy is going to be either to accept any job offer, $\sigma_{LH}$, or to accept only the high wage job offer, $\sigma_{H}$.

Let's analyze the $\sigma_H$ strategy:

$$\begin{aligned} V^{\sigma_H}(N) &= 0 + \delta\left[\tfrac{1}{3}V^{\sigma_H}(N) + \tfrac{1}{3}V^{\sigma_H}(L) + \tfrac{1}{3}V^{\sigma_H}(H)\right]\\[6pt] V^{\sigma_H}(L) &= 0 + \delta\left[\tfrac{1}{3}V^{\sigma_H}(N) + \tfrac{1}{3}V^{\sigma_H}(L) + \tfrac{1}{3}V^{\sigma_H}(H)\right]\\[6pt] V^{\sigma_H}(H) &= \frac{20}{1-\delta} \end{aligned}$$

Solving this linear system of equations we obtain:

$$V^{\sigma_H}(N) = V^{\sigma_H}(L) = \frac{20\delta}{(1-\delta)(3-2\delta)} \quad\text{and}\quad V^{\sigma_H}(H) = \frac{20}{1-\delta}$$

We only need to check for one-shot deviations at state $L$ (deviating to stop):

$$V^{\sigma_H}(L) \geq u(L,s) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{20\delta}{(1-\delta)(3-2\delta)} \geq \frac{10}{1-\delta} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \delta \geq \tfrac{3}{4}$$

In sum, more patient agents ($\delta \geq \tfrac{3}{4}$) will only accept the high wage job offer, while more impatient agents ($\delta \lt \tfrac{3}{4}$) will accept any job offer. The expected unemployment duration is longer for patient agents ($2$ periods) than for impatient ones ($\tfrac{1}{2}$ period).
