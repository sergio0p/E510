Dynamic Games

Strategies are not equivalent to actions

In dynamic games, a player's strategy must specify an action for each of the nodes the player controls. In other words, a strategy for a player is a function from the history of the game (the sequence of actions taken so far) to the action the player will take at each of her or his decision nodes.

In the previous game while the general strategy coincides with his actions since he only moves once, the president strategy must specify what to do when the general chose to "nuke USSR" and what to do when the general choose to "not nuke". Thus, the President has four strategies:

"don't nuke" if the General nukes, "nuke USSR" if the General doesn't

Backward Induction

A Recursive Method

  • Start at the final period $T$. The last mover chooses an action to maximize their payoff given the history of play. Solve for the optimal action as a function of that history.
  • The solution at period $T$ defines the value of the subgame starting at $T$. This value depends on the actions taken in earlier periods.
  • Move to period $T-1$. The player moving at $T-1$ anticipates how the player at $T$ will respond. Substitute the optimal action from $T$ into the $T-1$ player's objective function.
  • The $T-1$ player now faces a reduced problem: maximize their payoff where the continuation value (from $T$ onward) is already determined by the subgame solution.

Backward Induction

A Recursive Method (cont.)

  • Repeat: solve for the optimal action at $T-1$, which defines the value of the subgame starting at $T-1$. Move to $T-2$ and continue.
  • At each step, the current player's utility incorporates the value of all future subgames, treating future optimal play as given.
  • The process terminates at period $1$, yielding the subgame perfect equilibrium path.

Backward Induction

Games With A Continuum of Actions

How do we find the subgame perfect equilibrium when players choose from a continuum of actions—as in the public-good, peer-effects, and tournament games we saw earlier?

  1. At the final nodes, apply Recipe #0 or Recipe #1 to find the last mover’s best response.
  2. Step back to the nodes immediately before them. Substitute the best response you just found into the payoff of the player who moves there. The new payoff depends on current and past actions only—the future actions have been eliminated.
  3. Apply Recipe #0 or Recipe #1 to this new payoff to obtain that player’s best response.
  4. Repeat, moving back one stage at a time, until you reach the initial node—the game is solved.

An Example

Peer Effects

Recall the peer-effects model. Individuals $1$ and $2$ choose actions $x$ and $y$. Each would like to choose her own favorite action, $a_1$ and $a_2$ respectively, but also dislikes choosing an action far from her peer’s, with $\lambda\gt 0$ measuring how heavily that peer pressure weighs:

$$\begin{aligned} u_1(x,y) &= 1-(a_1-x)^2-\lambda\,(y-x)^2\\[2pt] u_2(x,y) &= 1-(a_2-y)^2-\lambda\,(x-y)^2 \end{aligned}$$

The simultaneous game, as a reminder. Each individual takes her peer’s action as given, so individual 1 maximizes $u_1$ over $x$ holding $y$ fixed:

$$\diff{}{x}u_1=2\,(a_1-x)+2\lambda\,(y-x)=0 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; x=BR_1(y)=\frac{a_1+\lambda\,y}{1+\lambda}$$

and symmetrically $y=BR_2(x)=\dfrac{a_2+\lambda\,x}{1+\lambda}$. The Nash equilibrium of the static game is the pair at which both best responses hold at once:

$$x^e=\frac{(1+\lambda)\,a_1+\lambda\,a_2}{1+2\lambda} \qquad\text{and}\qquad y^e=\frac{(1+\lambda)\,a_2+\lambda\,a_1}{1+2\lambda}$$

Now change the timing. Individual 1 chooses $x$ first. Individual 2 observes $x$ and only then chooses $y$. Nothing else changes—the same two utility functions, the same $\lambda$.

Individual 2’s strategy. A strategy must specify an action at every node the player controls. Individual 2 controls one node for each $x$ that individual 1 might pick, and every one of those nodes starts a subgame. Subgame perfection asks 2 to best respond in all of them—not only at the $x$ that actually gets played. So in any subgame perfect Nash equilibrium, individual 2’s strategy is the entire best-response function

$$y=BR_2(x)=\frac{a_2+\lambda\,x}{1+\lambda},$$

one action for every $x$. The single number 2 ends up playing is just the value of that function at the $x$ individual 1 chose.

Individual 1 knows this. She is not choosing against a fixed $y$—she is choosing a point along 2’s best-response function. Substituting 2’s strategy into her own utility,

$$u_1\paren{x,BR_2(x)}=1-(a_1-x)^2-\lambda\paren{\frac{a_2+\lambda\,x}{1+\lambda}-x}^2$$

which is a function of $x$ alone.

Apply Recipe #0. Differentiating with respect to $x$ by the chain rule and setting the derivative to zero,

$$\diff{}{x}u_1\paren{x,BR_2(x)} =2\,(a_1-x)-2\lambda\paren{\frac{a_2+\lambda\,x}{1+\lambda}-x}\paren{\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}-1}=0$$

The solution. Solving the first-order condition for $x$, and then feeding that $x$ back into 2’s strategy,

$$x^\ast=\frac{(1+\lambda)^2a_1+\lambda\,a_2}{1+3\lambda+\lambda^2} \qquad\text{and}\qquad y^\ast=BR_2(x^\ast)=\frac{(1+2\lambda)\,a_2+\lambda(1+\lambda)\,a_1}{1+3\lambda+\lambda^2}$$

This is not the static Nash equilibrium. Subtracting the static solution,

$$x^\ast-x^e=\frac{\lambda^2(1+\lambda)\,(a_1-a_2)}{(1+2\lambda)(1+3\lambda+\lambda^2)} \qquad\text{and}\qquad y^\ast-y^e=\frac{\lambda^3\,(a_1-a_2)}{(1+2\lambda)(1+3\lambda+\lambda^2)}$$

Both differences carry the sign of $a_1-a_2$: moving first, individual 1 commits closer to her own favorite action, and individual 2—who dislikes being far from her peer—follows her there. Individual 1 moves $\tfrac{1+\lambda}{\lambda}$ times as far as individual 2 does.

Two limiting cases make the point. At $\lambda=0$ both differences vanish: with no peer pressure there is nothing for individual 1 to exploit, and each simply plays her favorite action. As $\lambda$ grows large the simultaneous game drives both actions to the midpoint $\tfrac{a_1+a_2}{2}$, while the sequential game drives both to $a_1$—the more the follower cares about conforming, the more completely the leader dictates the outcome.

The first-mover advantage. Individual 1 is better off:

$$u_1(x^\ast,y^\ast)-u_1(x^e,y^e)=\frac{(a_1-a_2)^2\,\lambda^4}{(1+2\lambda)^2(1+3\lambda+\lambda^2)}\ge 0$$

and you can see why without computing anything. The static Nash pair $(x^e,y^e)$ also lies on 2’s best-response function, since $y^e=BR_2(x^e)$. So it was one of the points individual 1 could have chosen when she maximized along that function—and she did not choose it.

PS 05

Peer Effects

Instead of substituting $BR_2(x)$ into $u_1$, there is a second way to find individual 1’s choice: keep both $x$ and $y$ as choice variables and impose individual 2’s strategy as a constraint. That is a maximization problem with one equality constraint—so it is Recipe #1.

$$\max\limits_{\substack{x,y\\ \mathrm{subject\, to} \\ \\ y \;=\; BR_2(x)}} u_1(x,y)$$

A note on notation. Recipe #1 writes the Lagrange multiplier as $\lambda$, but here $\lambda$ is already taken—it is the weight of the peer pressure in $u_1$ and $u_2$. To avoid the collision we call the multiplier $l$, as you did in PS 02, and it is quicker to type.

  1. Write the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}$ corresponding to the problem.
  2. Obtain the gradient of the Lagrangian.
  3. Set the first-order conditions.
  4. Show the solution of the first-order conditions—the values of $x$, $y$ and $l$.

Hints. Make sure to use the actual expression for $BR_2(x)$, not the symbol. And notice that you have three variables—$x$, $y$ and $l$—when you compute the gradient and solve the first-order conditions.

Notice that $y$ is the action individual 2 plays in equilibrium—not individual 2’s strategy, which is the whole function $BR_2(x)$.

Problem Set 03

The set-up is identical to Problem Set 02.

Mark Watney is stranded on Mars but will be rescued after four periods. Time is discrete: $t=0,1,2,3$. He has only 4 potatoes (which we shall assume are infinitely divisible). We use $k_t$ to denote his stock of potatoes at the beginning of period $t$, so the stock starts at $k_0=4$.

At every period he decides how many potatoes to consume, which we denote by $c_t$, and then the stock of potatoes grows according to the rule $$k_t = 2\cdot\paren{k_{t-1}-c_{t-1}}.$$ In words, the amount of unconsumed potatoes doubles. Mark’s utility in period $t$ is given by $\sqrt{c_t}$ and he discounts future utility according to the discount factor $\delta=0.8$.

In order to solve this problem it will be easier if we write it in terms of the amount of potatoes that are saved (not consumed) in period $t$: $$s_t=k_t-c_t.$$ Thus $c_t=k_t-s_t$ and also $k_t=2\cdot s_{t-1}$ (by the growth law of potatoes).

Notice we can solve this by finding the subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium of the game with one player. Let the utility of the player in period $t$ be $\mathbf{U[t]}$. For example $$\mathbf{U[3]=0.8^3*Sqrt[c[3]]}$$ as the past utility is sunk.
